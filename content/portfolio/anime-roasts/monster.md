Right, so there's this cartoon called Monster. It’s about a doctor, a real top-shelf brain surgeon, who makes the rookie mistake of doing his job too well.

His name is Kenzo Tenma, and one day he's faced with a choice: save the life of a very important mayor, or save the life of some random kid with a bullet in his head. Being a doctor, he chooses the kid, because apparently, "all lives are equal" is something he took seriously from the brochure.

Turns out, the kid he saves grows up to be Johan, a blond sociopath with the charisma of a golden retriever and the moral compass of a particularly ambitious virus. Johan's main hobby is whispering sweet nothings into people's ears until they commit elaborate murder-suicides. He's basically a walking, talking internet comments section.

So Tenma, consumed by the world's most severe case of buyer's remorse, abandons his career, his fiancée, and his promising future to go on a backpacking trip across Germany to find this kid and, you know, shoot him. A classic post-op follow-up.

What follows is 74 episodes of Tenma being the worst fugitive in history. He travels across Europe, leaving a trail of emotionally damaged people and convenient clues. Every person he meets has a backstory that's at least three episodes long and ends with them either dying tragically or giving him a cryptic piece of advice before he catches the next train.

Meanwhile, there's Inspector Lunge, a man whose entire personality is that he's really good at remembering things and has a weird air-typing habit. He's convinced Tenma is the killer because it’s statistically more probable than "a ghost-like twink is convincing everyone to die." It’s a solid theory, if you ignore all the evidence.

The show tries to be very deep, asking big questions like, "What is evil?" "Can a person's name define them?" and "How many orphanages can one evil East German science experiment possibly create?" It meanders through Cold War conspiracies, secret eugenics programmes, and a children’s picture book that apparently doubles as a serial killer training manual.

For a thriller, it moves at the speed of a glacier. You'll watch five episodes where the main plot development is that Tenma finds a slightly older photograph of Johan. You could learn German, get a medical degree, and solve a few actual cold cases in the time it takes to finish this series.

In the end, after dozens of hours of brooding, philosophical monologues, and near-misses, we learn that the real monster… was probably the friends we made along the way. Or the human condition. Or maybe it was just Johan. The show's very ambiguous about it, which is a clever way of saying they didn't really know how to end it.

So, yeah. Monster. Come for the ethical dilemma, stay because you've invested 30 hours and the sunk cost fallacy has you in a chokehold. 10/10, if you have a spare lifetime.
