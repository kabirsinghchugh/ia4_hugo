Right. Attack on Titan.

So, you’ve decided to get into a story about a long-running and rather loud disagreement over property lines, complicated by the fact that one side is a collection of giant, naked cannibals with terrible posture. It’s basically a homeowners' association dispute if the HOA was made up of enormous, smiling toddlers who eat you for leaving your bins out.

Let’s break down the key players in this extended municipal crisis.

First, you have The Protagonist. His name is Eren. His primary personality trait is shouting. His hobbies include shouting, being angry, and occasionally turning into one of the giant naked people, but an even angrier version with better abs. His character arc is a mesmerising journey from "I must kill all my enemies" to "Wait, what if everyone is my enemy?" which, you have to admit, is an efficient way to solve the first problem.

Then you have his support staff.

 * Mikasa: Her entire operating system runs on a single file named Eren.exe. If that file is not in her immediate vicinity, she will find it. Her function is to be incredibly good at violence and to say "Eren" with varying levels of concern. She is motivated by a scarf. It’s a whole thing.
 * Armin: He is the brains of the operation, which means his main job is to have a strategic epiphany, explain it to everyone, and then cry about it. He is the group's designated feeler of feelings. Later on, his job description is upgraded to include "becoming a walking, talking tactical nuke," which is, you know, quite the promotion.

And of course, you have Management. This is mostly Levi. He's a man whose profound emotional depth is matched only by his profound shortness. He’s a weaponised fidget spinner fuelled by tea and a deep-seated hatred for dust. His management style is kicking subordinates and making everyone feel inadequate. He’s very popular.

The plot is simple, really.

Phase One: Pest Control. A big wall has a hole in it. The pests get in. Everyone screams. A lot. The goal is to plug the hole and exterminate the pests. A surprising number of people die in workplace accidents involving being bitten in half. It’s very sub-optimal for morale. They spend about three seasons trying to get into a basement, which has to be the most overhyped cellar in narrative history.

Phase Two: The Paperwork. They get into the basement. They discover the world is much bigger and hates them for reasons involving historical grievances and bad press. The show then pivots from a monster-slaying adventure into a deeply depressing geopolitical thriller. The screaming continues, but now it’s about war crimes and ethical quandaries.

Phase Three: Global Unfriending. Eren decides that the best way to end racism is to eliminate all the other races. It's a bold strategy. He activates the "Rumbling," which is the planetary equivalent of flipping the table over during a board game you're losing. A herd of colossal giants walks across the earth to methodically stomp on everything. It’s a very thorough, if somewhat unsubtle, approach to foreign policy.

And the message?

Well, it seems to be that violence is bad, but also the only thing that works. Freedom is important, but it mostly leads to more violence. Humanity is a cycle of hatred, but maybe if we kill 90% of it, the remaining 10% will be nice to each other for a bit. The ending suggests that the real solution might involve a bird. It's unclear.

So there you have it. Attack on Titan. Come for the cool 3D-manoeuvring gear, stay for the crushing existential despair and generational trauma. It’s a fun time, if your idea of fun is a 90-hour anxiety attack with a side of political science homework.
