You know how sometimes you join a new company - it's a start-up,  good camaraderie, lots of travel, and the CEO is this impossibly charismatic guy with fantastic hair. You work hard, you play hard, you climb the corporate ladder, and you think you're finally going places.

Then, one day, during the annual company retreat, your CEO sacrifices the entire company as a buffet to extra-dimensional demons, for that big promotion to join the board of directors - just another Tuesday in Silicon Valley. 

Which brings us to Berserk.

Berserk is a heartwarming slice-of-life story about a man named Guts, whose main personality trait is having the worst day of his life, every day of his life, for decades.

His best friend and boss is a man named Griffith, who proves that the most dangerous thing in the universe is a man who says he would do anything to reach his goals, which is you know, admirable, until you ask what he meant by “anything”.

The story of Berserk revolves around their love-hate relationship. Let's break it down

1. The Protagonist: Guts. 

Imagine you stubbed your toe, and instead of hopping around for a minute, you dedicated the rest of your existence to waging a one-man war against the very concept of furniture. That's Guts. His problem-solving flowchart has one box, and inside it says, "Swing the giant slab of iron." 

> Is the problem a demon? 
> > Swing the iron. 

> Is it a political intrigue? 
> > Swing the iron. 

> Is it an existential crisis about the nature of causality? 
> > You'd better goddamn believe you swing that iron. 

Guts carries so much trauma he probably has to pay extra baggage fees in the afterlife.


2. The Antagonist/Best Friend/Reason for Everything: Griffith. 

Griffith is the man who looked at the trolley problem and thought, "Why not just run over everyone, including the person who built the tracks, and then use the trolley to build myself a castle?" 

He is ambition with a human face. Although, a very, very pretty face. 

His betrayal isn't just a backstab; it's a front-stab, side-stab, and an aerial-bombardment-stab while your soul is being repossessed. 

He's the friend who borrows a tenner and pays you back by erasing your corporeal form from the canons of reality.

3. The Supporting Cast

You have Casca, a character who drew the shortest straw in the history of literature and then got beaten with it.

Then you have the Band of the Hawk, a highly effective group of mercenaries whose main strategic function was to stand around and be really impressed by Griffith right up until they were converted into an all-you-can-eat buffet for interdimensional nightmares.

4. The World

The setting of Berserk is a place that makes Mordor look like a sensible holiday destination. Every forest has a demon, every castle has a pervert, and every village has a plague. 

If you see a cute animal, it's probably a vessel for a primordial evil.

The main export of the world of Berserk is suffering, and boy, isn’t the business booming.

The overarching plot is nuanced (Sorry ai flaggers - i used the n word), exploration of free-will, causality, and human resilience.  Or, it's about a very-very angry man slashing things with a seven-foot-long lump of pig iron until the problem goes away, until the next, worse problem arrives.

So, if your friend tells you that Berserk is a masterpiece of dark fantasy, what they're trying to say is that you're about to spend the next several weeks watching a man with a metal arm punch a horse-demon so hard its ancestors feel it.

And that's basically it. It's a story about the power of friendship, if your definition of friendship is broad enough to include being branded for eternal damnation by your mate so he can get a corner office in hell.
