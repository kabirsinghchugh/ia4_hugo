## Why _Gintama_ Is the Greatest Comedy Anime of All Time

If you’ve never watched _Gintama_, imagine an anime that looks like a standard Shounen Jump series, except the main character is a lazy samurai who eats sugar like it’s cocaine, the supporting cast includes a girl who can punch through concrete but still gets motion sickness, and an alien dog the size of a car who is somehow less weird than everyone else. That’s _Gintama_.

At first glance, it seems like a regular action-comedy about samurai in space-Japan. But then you realise the plot is basically an excuse for the writers to do whatever they want, whenever they want. For example, one episode is a serious political drama about rebellion, the next is about hemorrhoids, and then the following week the main characters are fighting over a stolen pair of panties with the same intensity as the final battle of _Attack on Titan_.

The genius of _Gintama_ is that it somehow makes no sense and perfect sense at the same time. Like, imagine if _The Simpsons_ had a crossover with _Naruto_, directed by Quentin Tarantino, and then someone added fart jokes. That’s basically every arc of _Gintama_.

Now, you might be wondering: how can a show with 300+ episodes of nonsense actually be considered one of the greatest anime of all time? Well, here’s the thing. While most comedies throw in a heartwarming moment or two to make you feel something, _Gintama_ takes it way too far. One second you’re laughing at a character who is literally named “Catherine” but is also a chain-smoking alien cat burglar, and the next second you’re crying over the tragic backstory of a ninja who can’t cook fried rice. It’s emotional whiplash, but in a good way, like going to a funeral and finding out there’s free beer.

The characters are written so well that you can genuinely care about them, even though 90% of the time they’re complete idiots. Gintoki, for example, is simultaneously the laziest protagonist in anime history and one of the most heroic. He will risk his life to protect his friends, but only if he’s done reading _Shonen Jump_ first. Kagura, despite being an alien with super strength, spends most of her time eating pickled seaweed and bullying everyone. Shinpachi is the “straight man” of the group, but his main character trait is that he wears glasses, which is apparently enough to get 300 episodes of screen time.

And then there’s the humour itself. _Gintama_ doesn’t just break the fourth wall; it demolishes it, builds a new one, and then breaks that too. The show has parodied literally every popular anime: _Dragon Ball_, _One Piece_, _Naruto_, _Bleach_. Sometimes in the same episode. At one point, the characters even complain about the animation budget on-screen, which is probably the most relatable thing in anime production history.

But the real magic is that when _Gintama_ decides to be serious, it suddenly transforms into one of the best dramas you’ve ever seen. Epic sword fights, political intrigue, gut-punching emotional arcs—you name it. It’s like if _Family Guy_ suddenly turned into _Game of Thrones_ halfway through an episode, but somehow it works.

So why is _Gintama_ the greatest comedy anime of all time? Because it’s not just comedy. It’s a perfectly unbalanced cocktail of parody, drama, slapstick, and sincerity. It’s stupid and brilliant, chaotic and precise, tragic and hilarious—all at once.

In other words, it’s the only anime where you can cry your eyes out at a tragic death scene and, five minutes later, laugh uncontrollably at a joke about nose hair. And if that’s not the definition of peak storytelling, then honestly, I don’t know what is.

## Why _Gintama_ is not just the greatest comedy anime — it’s a practical philosophy you accidentally subscribe to

You think you like comedy anime. You don’t. What you like is safe, metric-tested, laugh-track-friendly comedy. _Gintama_ isn’t that. It’s an anarchic long-form experiment that starts as ridiculous sketch-comedy, then gradually — and deliberately — turns into one of the toughest character dramas anime will let you cry at, and it never loses the jokes along the way. That contradiction is the point.

Start with the backbone: Gintoki, Kagura and Shinpachi are the “straight trio” who sell the absurdity like pros. Gintoki is a slacker samurai whose life philosophy could be condensed to: “I’ll help you, but I’m asking for my weekly sugar fix first.” The trio anchor the show’s tonal elasticity — they can deliver a fart gag in the morning and an existential confrontation at night, and both land because we’ve already accepted them as real people who are also walking punchlines.

Now look at the supporting cast. This is where _Gintama_’s comedy becomes durable, not disposable. Take Hijikata: chain-smoking, ice-cold vice-commander who has the nation’s most unhealthy devotion — to mayonnaise. The gag isn’t a single beat; it metastasises into entire episodes, props, lore and an actual personality trait that informs battles and heartache. It’s absurdity turned into character, which is why it keeps being funny even after a hundred jokes about it.

Or take Taizō Hasegawa, aka “MADAO” — a recurring running gag that became a tragicomic study of failure. Kagura invents the nickname, it sticks, and the joke is a social diagnosis: “MADAO” roughly means ‘completely hopeless old man’. It’s cruel and affectionate, and _Gintama_ treats it as both punchline and social commentary. You laugh, then you feel bad, then you laugh again because the show is allergic to single-tone emotions.

The show’s side characters are not one-off sketches: Prince Hata (the idiot alien with exotic pets), the ever-masked Katsura (who is what happens when a freedom fighter gets into cosplay), the Shinsengumi (who are simultaneously honourable and ridiculous) — every recurring gag becomes a lived-in habit. The Prince’s pet disasters, Katsura’s disguises and Hasegawa’s downward drift are not lazily repeated; they accumulate meaning.

Absurdist gold: the writers take other anime and strip them for parts. There’s a straight-up parody planet called Hamek — a direct send-up of DBZ’s Namek — which, instead of dragon balls, is famous for tobacco fields and a people devoted to cigarettes. The gag functions on two levels: it’s an immediate parody and it’s used to stage Hijikata’s nicotine desperation as an interplanetary quest. That silliness becomes a coherent micro-plot, and then it feeds back into Hijikata’s character arcs. Parody is never lazy here; it’s additive.

About running gags: _Gintama_ weaponises overuse. The “Katsura wig” gag, the “die, Hijikata!” shouts, the endless ladders of humiliation heaped on Madao — they’re funny because repetition converts a joke into culture. When a gag is repeated until it’s “unfunny,” _Gintama_ refuses to retire it; instead, it evolves the gag into a worldview. Within the show, these refrains become social norms. People talk about them like habits; they define relationships. That’s where the surreal humour becomes existential.

And then the show hits you with stakes. _Gintama_’s dramatic spine is a slow burn about betrayal, revenge and the ghosts of a lost war. The Benizakura arc is the pivot: it introduces Takasugi and the Kiheitai, gives the first real taste of the trio’s shared history with their teacher Shōyō, and proves the writers can handle ferocious drama without betraying the comedy DNA. Takasugi is not “a moustache-twisting villain”; he’s a warped reflection of Gintoki — same past, opposite answer to trauma. The revenge narrative intensifies into something tragic and human, and the anime doesn’t back away from consequences.

Signature highlights — the show’s headline ridiculousness and its genuine emotional peaks — exist side-by-side:

- The “Chome-Chome” songs and obscene pop parodies: juvenile, filthy, bleep-filled and absolutely hilarious because the timing and performance are immaculate. These are the surface-level filthy jokes that stick in your head and annoy your sensibilities on purpose.
- Weatherwoman episodes and similar oddities: an apparently trivial gag about a weather presenter (episode arcs where the forecast is supernatural) that the series expands into a mini-mythology and a character beat. The show makes even filler feel like world-building.
- Benizakura, Yoshiwara in Flames, Shinsengumi Crisis and later Tendōshū/ Utsuro arcs: the series’ highest-rated, most-discussed emotional arcs. These are pure spectacle and gut-punch combined. Fans point to them as the moment _Gintama_ proves its dramatic competence.

There’s also self-deprecation in _Gintama_ that most anime won’t touch. The show openly mocks its own animation budget, its genre, its tropes, even the idea that it could be “the greatest anime.” That reflexive humility does two things: it wins cheap laughs, and it lowers the stakes so when the stakes rise, you’re actually surprised. That difference between how the series sells you the joke and how it delivers the tragedy is what makes the serious moments land harder.

One last, crucial point: the series quality graph is not a straight decline. People who start and quit early usually claim it “goes downhill.” That’s because _Gintama_ intentionally takes time to teach you its rhythms — the payoff is back-loaded. By the time the major arcs hit, the animation, the voice work, the pacing and the writing converge; the show gets legitimately better and more confident. The fandom consensus is blunt: it grows on you and then repays patience with some of the best emotional payoffs in shonen-adjacent fiction.

So why call it one of the greatest anime, not just the funniest? Because comedy here is a scaffolding, not a genre. The show builds character, world and tragedy out of jokes, and because everything in _Gintama_ is earned — even the dumb jokes — the emotional beats aren’t tacked on. They are the inevitable consequence of being absurdly, painfully human for 300 episodes.

If you want a single test: watch the Benizakura arc, then watch the “mayonnaise factory” Hijikata episodes, and tell me those two tonal poles couldn’t belong to the same piece of art. If you still disagree, fine. You’re allowed to be wrong.
