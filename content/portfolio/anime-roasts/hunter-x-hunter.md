Right. Let's talk about the show where a twelve-year-old in questionable green shorts decides the best way to deal with his crippling abandonment issues is to get the same dangerous gig-worker license that made his dad leave in the first place. This is Hunter x Hunter.

Our story follows Gon, a simple boy from a simple island whose life goal is to track down his father. A man who, upon discovering he had a son, decided that cataloguing undiscovered species of magical beasts was a more compelling use of his time than, say, parenting. To find this world-class deadbeat, Gon decides to become a Hunter, which is less of a job and more of a heavily-armed library card that lets you go anywhere and do basically anything, including casual war crimes, without anyone batting an eye.

Naturally, a child cannot go on a quest to confront his absentee father alone. He needs a support group.

First, we have Killua. He’s also twelve and running away from his family business. The family business is being comically evil assassins. Killua’s childhood was a bit different from most. Instead of learning to ride a bike, he was learning to become immune to poison and how to rip a man’s heart out with his bare hands. He’s trying to escape this life for a normal childhood, which he seems to think involves life-or-death battles alongside his new best friend. To each their own.

Then there’s Kurapika. He’s the last of his kind because a group of thieves with a spider tattoo thought his clan’s glowing red eyes would make for a great addition to their macabre collection. His entire personality now revolves around retrieving these eyeballs. He is less a person and more a walking, chain-wielding lost-and-found department for human organs.

And finally, we have Leorio. He’s a man who looks thirty, acts fifty, and claims to be a teenager. He wants to be a doctor to help people, but mostly so he can be obscenely rich. He is the group's designated adult, chauffeur, and primary source of loud complaining.

The central pillar of their world is a power system called Nen. It's your life force, your aura. It's also so needlessly complicated that the show regularly stops dead for twenty minutes so a narrator can deliver a university-level lecture on its mechanics, complete with diagrams. You spend half an arc learning the rules, only for some guy to show up whose power is to conjure a vacuum cleaner that sucks up ghosts. It's all very intuitive.

The plot unfolds in a series of family-friendly adventures.

First, they take the Hunter Exam, a job interview where the failure rate is around 99.9% and often involves being eaten by a large swamp creature. It's a bit like applying for an unpaid internship, but with a higher chance of dismemberment.

Then there’s the part where Killua goes home for a bit, and his family welcomes him back by chaining him to a wall in their dungeon. It’s tough love.

After that, they go to a big tower and punch people for money until they learn about the magic lecture system, Nen.

Next up is Yorknew City, where Kurapika tracks down the spider-themed art collectors to reclaim his people's eyeballs. Meanwhile, Gon and Killua try to raise money by using their newfound life-force energy to appraise antiques. It’s a touching story about friendship, vengeance, and the cut-throat world of high-stakes auctioning.

But the real highlight is the Chimera Ant arc. An arc so long it has its own distinct geological era. It starts simply: giant ants eat people and take on their traits. It ends with a philosophical debate between a moth-man and a cat-humanoid about the meaning of one's name, while the king of the ants, a being of unimaginable power, discovers his true purpose in life is to get really, really good at board games. It’s a gruelling, 60-episode-long meditation on humanity that is occasionally interrupted by extreme violence. The narrator works overtime here, explaining every thought, every feeling, every micro-expression, just in case you, the viewer, were incapable of object permanence.

After that marathon of existential dread, the story pivots to... a student council election. With just as much tension and strategic manoeuvring.

And that’s pretty much where we are. On a boat. For years. A significant portion of the fanbase has likely been born, learned to walk, and graduated from university in the time the main characters have been on this boat.

So if you’re looking for a sprawling epic about a boy’s quest for his father, with a deep, intricate power system, and a guarantee that the story will probably finish sometime before the heat death of the universe, well, you've found it. Maybe. No promises.
