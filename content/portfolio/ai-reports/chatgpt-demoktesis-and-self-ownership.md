## **Demoktesis: The Ultimate Ipo of the Self? a Thought Experiment on Freedom, Ownership, and the State**

Let's embark on a mind-bending journey, following Nozick's Demoktesis, where we take a simple idea—what if we could issue shares in ourselves?—to its logical, unsettling conclusion. This isn't just about government or philosophy; it's about you, your autonomy, and whether you'd still be free if everyone else owned a piece of your decision-making.

---

## **1. The First Share: a Revolutionary Idea**

Imagine you're short on cash. You hit upon a brilliant idea: why not sell a share in yourself? You divide your rights—what job you take, where you live, whether you smoke, what books you read—into discrete shares, just like a company does when it goes public. At first, it's a novelty, a game. Maybe you sell 5% of your voting rights over personal decisions. Investors get a say in your life choices, but you keep control over the big stuff.

So far, it seems harmless. But demand grows.

People want to buy shares in others, too—after all, if you own a vote in someone's life, you might influence their productivity, their well-being, and, in turn, increase your return on investment. A vibrant stock market emerges, where people trade shares in each other, some focusing on decision-making rights over lifestyle choices, others on financial decisions. You still have a say, but now you're accountable to your shareholders.

---

## **2. The Stock Market of the Self**

As more people enter the market, specialized firms emerge. "Life Management Funds" offer diversified portfolios: 10% of Alice's career decisions, 3% of Bob's dating choices, and a controlling interest in Charlie's diet. People still have nominal ownership over themselves, but their choices are increasingly constrained by shareholder votes.

We enter a new phase: **corporate governance of individuals**. Boards of directors form for high-value individuals, making their decisions more efficiently. Your life choices are now subject to shareholder meetings. You attend quarterly reviews where your investors vote on major aspects of your existence.

Some people try to retain control by holding onto a majority stake in themselves. Others, in desperate need of cash or hoping to optimize their lives, sell off controlling interest.

And then, something fascinating happens: consolidations.

---

## **3. The Merging of Individuals: from Ownership to Collectivization**

A problem emerges. Managing individual life-shares is inefficient. What if, instead of each person managing their own share structure, we consolidated ownership into a **single, streamlined corporate entity**?

Enter the **Great Corporation**—a democratic, shareholder-controlled entity where everyone owns exactly **one** share in every other person.

The transition is logical. Instead of thousands of stockholder meetings about individual life choices, one central authority now determines major social and economic decisions. Rather than isolated, inefficient markets, we get an organized system. Now, **everyone owns everyone, equally**.

And here's the kicker: **each person still owns exactly one share of  
themselves.**

Nozick argues, ironically, that this seems like a fair trade. After all, each individual is in precisely the same position as everyone else—no one has more control over another. The vast, democratic collective votes on what's permissible in each other's lives. Major life decisions—what you eat, how much of your income you keep, what books you read—are subject to democratic deliberation.

And, crucially, because everyone owns everyone, **no one owns anyone**.

Or so it seems.

---

## **4. The Reality of "Demoktesis": the Slave by Another Name**

Nozick points out a chilling parallel: this "perfectly voluntary" collectivization **mirrors the structure of the modern state**. If we take an individual and gradually add democratic control, does there come a point where they stop being free?

To illustrate this, Nozick presents **The Tale of the Slave**:

1. A slave is entirely under his master's control, punished for disobedience.
2. The master becomes kinder, beating him only for breaking stated rules.
3. The master allows some free time but retains control over major aspects of  
   the slave's life.
4. The slave can now earn money but must give a fixed portion back to the  
   master.
5. Instead of one master, a group of 10,000 now votes on how the slave must  
   behave.
6. The slave is allowed to participate in discussions but cannot vote.
7. He is given one vote, but it is only counted in a tie.
8. His vote is always included, but it never changes an outcome.

At which step did he **stop being a slave**?

The disturbing implication: when a person is entirely subject to collective decision-making, even in a "democratic" system, at what point do they lose autonomy? **Demoktesis—government by ownership—raises the question of whether democracy itself is just a polite form of servitude.**

---

## **5. The Hidden Implication: the State as a Universal Shareholder**

'Nozick's thought experiment exposes something deep about the nature of modern states. In a democratic system, the government dictates parts of our lives—taxation, laws, personal behaviors—not unlike a stockholder controlling a company.'

If the government **owns** part of you, then **to what extent are you still  
free**?

Demoktesis suggests that democracy might not be fundamentally different from authoritarianism. The difference is merely that **the power is dispersed** among many rather than concentrated in one.

And if you think this is far-fetched, consider taxation, compulsory jury duty, military drafts, or even regulations on personal choices. Nozick's point is that **if people "voluntarily" enter a system where they trade autonomy for security, it doesn't necessarily stop being coercion**.

Even in an egalitarian, collective democracy, the core issue remains: **do you truly own yourself? Or does the system own you?**

---

## **6. The Final Question: Freedom, Ownership, and the Nature of the State**

So, if everyone owns a share in you, and you own a share in everyone, does that mean **no one is truly free**, or that **everyone is equally free**?

Nozick doesn't give a definitive answer. He invites you to think. He forces us to ask: **is democracy just a sophisticated form of universal partial slavery, where we are all both master and servant?**

More importantly, is there an alternative?

Is true freedom possible in a system where others have a claim over your decisions?

---

## **7. Closing Thoughts: is there a way Out?**

Nozick leaves us with an uncomfortable realization: the problem isn't democracy per se, but **the structure of collective ownership over individual autonomy**.

One could argue that **the only truly free society is one where no one owns any part of anyone else**—where decisions are entirely individual, and no one has the right to dictate another's choices. This would lead to something close to **anarcho-libertarianism**, where each person is their own master.

Or, we might accept that **some level of collective control is inevitable**, but then we must carefully define its limits. How much of yourself are you willing to sell, whether to a literal stock market or to the democratic state?

And if the answer is **anything greater than zero**, Nozick's question lingers:

**At what point do you stop being free?**

## **Demoktesis Revisited: a Formal Economic Analysis of Freedom, Ownership, and the State**

## **Introduction: Beyond the Narrative**

In our last discussion, we walked through Robert Nozick's **Demoktesis** thought experiment, illustrating how a voluntary system of selling decision rights over oneself can logically lead to **a universal, democratic ownership structure**.

We reached the unsettling conclusion that democracy, under such conditions, can resemble a sophisticated form of mutual servitude—where everyone owns a share of everyone, and thus, no one is truly free.

Today, we will **formalize the discussion**, analyzing **Demoktesis from an  
economic perspective**. We will break down its implications using:

1. **Game Theory** – How rational agents behave in this system.
2. **Property Rights Economics** – The efficiency of selling self-ownership.
3. **Public Choice Theory** – The final emergence of the state.
4. **Political Economy** – Where this fits in theories of governance and  
   liberty.

Let's get started.

---

## **1. Game-theoretic Analysis: the Rational Decision to Sell Oneself**

Let's consider an individual's decision to sell **shares in their own  
decision-making process** as a strategic move. Each person has a set of  
**self-ownership rights** $S_i$ that they can choose to retain or sell.

## **1.1 The Incentives to Sell**

An individual facing economic hardship may rationally decide to **liquidate  
portions of their autonomy** to raise capital. Suppose a person values their autonomy at $U(S_i)$, where $U$ is their utility function over  
self-ownership.

If an investor offers **a price $P$ for a fraction $\alpha$ of the  
individual's rights**, the decision problem becomes:

$$
  
\max \ U(S_i - \alpha S_i) + P  
$$

The individual will sell if:

$$
  
P > U(S_i) - U(S_i - \alpha S_i)  
$$

That is, if the price outweighs the **marginal disutility of losing that  
fraction of autonomy**.

### **Equilibrium Outcome**

- **Short-term:** Individuals voluntarily participate in the market.
- **Long-term:** As more people sell, market forces incentivize  
  consolidation—first into **majority holdings by powerful investors**, then into **collective ownership structures** (the Great Corporation in Nozick's scenario).

A Nash Equilibrium emerges when all individuals, in response to the aggregate market behavior, have no incentive to deviate from **universal ownership by the collective**. Once the transition happens, any attempt to reclaim self-ownership means incurring massive coordination costs against an already **established governance structure**.

At that stage, self-ownership is practically gone, **even if every step was voluntary**.

---

## **2. Property Rights Economics: the Inefficiency of Self-Selling**

## **2.1 Coase Theorem and Demoktesis**

The **Coase Theorem** tells us that, in the presence of well-defined property rights and zero transaction costs, voluntary exchanges will lead to **Pareto-efficient outcomes**. But does this hold for self-ownership?

In the Demoktesis scenario, each person's **decision rights become a tradable asset**. However, Nozick's argument suggests that, despite voluntary transactions, the final **equilibrium is deeply problematic**. Why?

1. **Fragmentation of decision-making rights**:

   - A single person making their own choices is **internally coherent**.
   - A collective **shareholding system of personal decisions** leads to **inefficiency**.
   - The classic "tragedy of the commons" applies: shareholders of your life have **diffuse accountability**, making it **costly** to coordinate optimal decisions.

2. **The Externality of Selling Self-Ownership**:
   - A person who sells decision rights today imposes **future costs** on themselves and others.
   - If **others** sell themselves into Demoktesis, it **pressures non-participants to join**, making opting out **increasingly expensive**.
   - Eventually, **a centralized democratic system is the only stable outcome**.

Thus, while each transaction might be **voluntarily efficient**, the **aggregate system is deeply inefficient**.

---

## **3. Public Choice Theory: the Rise of the Collective Leviathan**

Public Choice Theory, pioneered by Buchanan and Tullock, examines how collective decision-making works when **individuals act out of self-interest**. How does this apply to Demoktesis?

## **3.1 Rational Ignorance and Shareholder Decision-Making**

In a world where everyone is both **a decision-maker over others** and **subject to collective votes**, we run into a classic **Public Choice problem**: **rational ignorance**.
- In a democracy, voters do not have **strong incentives to gather information**  
  about policies since their individual vote has **almost no impact**.
- In Demoktesis, where millions of people **own decision rights over millions of  
  others**, the **complexity of decision-making skyrockets**.
- Result? **Decisions become dominated by interest groups**—those who **organize  
  and coordinate effectively gain disproportionate control**.

Thus, despite the system being **democratic**, the final structure looks **no different from an oligarchy**.

## **3.2 The Rent-seeking Problem**

Once decisions are collectivized, **special interests** start to emerge. Some groups lobby for:

- Greater **decision-making power over others**.
- Restrictions that favor **certain voting blocs**.
- Policies that **transfer wealth and power** to dominant shareholder groups.

Nozick's point: **When everyone owns everyone, the system becomes  
indistinguishable from centralized state control.**

---

## **4. Political Economy: what Demoktesis Teaches Us about the State**

At this point, we must ask: **what is the actual difference between Demoktesis and modern government?** Nozick suggests **very little**.

## **4.1 Democracy as Mutual Enslavement?**

- In a democracy, individuals vote on laws **that restrict others' freedoms**.
- This creates an illusion of freedom—**everyone participates, but no one is truly autonomous**.
- The state, like the Great Corporation in Demoktesis, **claims legitimacy through majority rule**, but the individual's sovereignty is ultimately **compromised**.

The key insight:

>The more decision-making is collectivized, the less personal autonomy remains.

Nozick's critique is profound: **Even if democracy is preferable to  
dictatorship, it still limits self-ownership in ways that should make us  
uneasy.**

---

## **5. Conclusion: is there an Escape from Demoktesis?**

Let's summarize our findings:

1. **Game Theory** – The rational decision to sell self-ownership leads **inevitably** to collective control.
2. **Property Rights Economics** – The process is voluntary, yet **leads to an inefficient, bureaucratic ownership structure**.
3. **Public Choice Theory** – Even in a democracy, collective decision-making is **dominated by interest groups and rational ignorance**.
4. **Political Economy** – The final result mirrors **a modern state**, where **individual freedom is sacrificed for collective rule**.

## **The Final Question: how Do We Avoid This?**

If Nozick is right, **how do we maintain real individual freedom?** The answer lies in **limiting the collectivization of decision-making**.

- **Minimal state structures**, not all-encompassing democratic control. - **Strong protections on individual choice**, not majority-imposed decisions. - **Voluntary contracts with explicit opt-out mechanisms**, preventing a one-way path to collective control.

In short: **we must recognize that voluntary choices can, paradoxically, lead to involuntary systems.** And if we care about individual autonomy, we must be extremely cautious **about how much of our decision-making we place in the hands of the collective**.

## **Final Thought: the Most Subtle Form of Tyranny**

Nozick's Demoktesis warns us that the most **dangerous** loss of freedom is the one we **freely walk into**, believing it will make us better off.

If everyone owns everyone, and no one owns anyone, ask yourself:

**Who really holds the power?**
