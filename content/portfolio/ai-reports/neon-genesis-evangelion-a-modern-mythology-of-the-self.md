## **Neon Genesis Evangelion: a Modern Mythology of the Self**

### **Introduction**

_Neon Genesis Evangelion_ is the story of humanity's desperate struggle against enigmatic "Angels" in a post-apocalyptic world. Yet beneath the surface lies staggering complexity—one that interweaves myths of creation and destruction with the struggles of identity and belonging. This is a modern myth, one that grapples with the existential crises of our age.

Drawing on the frameworks of Freud, Jung, and Campbell, lets unravel the anime from a mythological and psychological dimensions. At its core, _Evangelion_ explores humanity's relationship with its origins, its unconscious drives, and the tension between individuality and collective existence. Through its symbolism, the series reimagines ancient archetypes for a world adrift in uncertainty

## **Mythology As Narrative Foundation**

### **Creation Myths and the Cosmic Conflict**

The story of _Neon Genesis Evangelion_ begins with the catastrophic "Second Impact," an event that echoes the cataclysms of mythic tradition. Like the biblical flood or the destruction of Atlantis, the Second Impact represents both punishment and purification, a resetting of humanity's hubristic trajectory. This disaster is traced to Adam and Lilith, entities that evoke a reimagining of biblical and Gnostic creation myths. Adam, initially envisioned as humanity's progenitor, becomes inert—a fallen god—while Lilith, the primordial feminine, supplants him as the source of life. Humanity's lineage, then, is not angelic but demonic, an inversion that forces the viewer to reconsider their place in a cosmos ruled by ambiguity rather than moral certainties.

The Angels, celestial beings tasked with bringing about the apocalypse, serve as adversaries in this unfolding drama. But their role transcends the mere physical; they embody the existential threat posed by the unknown. In Campbell's _Hero's Journey_, such beings are trials to be confronted, catalysts for transformation rather than mere destroyers. Here, the Angels' otherworldly nature compels humanity to question the righteousness of its own existence, making each encounter as much an inward battle as an external one.

### **The Gnostic Demiurge and the Fall**

At the heart of _Evangelion_ lies a cautionary tale of hubris: humanity, represented by the clandestine organization Nerv, seeks to replicate divine creation. The Evangelions—colossal biomechanical constructs made in the image of Adam—are both saviours and abominations. Gendo Ikari, Nerv's enigmatic leader, embodies the Gnostic Demiurge, a creator not of divine perfection but of a flawed, material realm. His relentless pursuit of control mirrors humanity's broader struggle to impose order on a chaotic universe, a folly that recalls the Kabbalistic concept of _tzimtzum_, where divine light withdraws to make space for independent existence. In _Evangelion_, this withdrawal is incomplete, leaving humanity trapped in a shadow of its intended divine form.

This fallen state resonates with Hindu notions of _maya_, the illusory material world that obscures higher truth. Just as Adam's inert form symbolizes divine potential corrupted by human interference, the Evangelions reveal humanity's inability to transcend its flawed origins. They are weapons born of desperation, wielded against a higher reality that remains incomprehensible.

### **Lilith, Adam, and the Inversion of Creation**

The mythological underpinnings of _Neon Genesis Evangelion_ delves into the murky waters of inversion—flipping traditional creation myths on their heads. Adam, traditionally the archetype of humanity's origin and divine favour, is rendered powerless and inert. Lilith, often demonized in biblical and Gnostic traditions, emerges as humanity's true progenitor. This inversion not only destabilizes familiar narratives but also forces a re-examination of humanity's relationship with divinity. Are we truly the image of God, as Genesis asserts, or are we the unintended consequence of a cosmic accident, as _Evangelion_ implies?

Lilith's role as progenitor recalls the duality she embodies in mythology. In biblical traditions, Lilith is both tempter and destroyer, while in Gnostic interpretations, she represents primal independence and defiance. Within the series, she symbolizes the feminine unconscious—the vast, libidinal, and uncontainable force that both creates and devours. Lilith's juxtaposition with Adam highlights an archetypal tension: the structured, paternal order of creation versus the chaotic, maternal abyss from which all life springs. This duality mirrors the Hindu interplay of Parvati, the nurturing mother, and Kali, the devouring goddess.

### **The Angels as Cosmic Archetypes**

The Angels, described as humanity's adversaries, are far from mere antagonists. Their incomprehensibility, their refusal to conform to human understanding, places them in the realm of archetypes rather than villains. Each Angel presents itself as a challenge to the characters, forcing them to confront their own fears, inadequacies, and the boundaries of their understanding. These are not creatures of evil but manifestations of humanity's estranged higher self.

In Jungian terms, the Angels can be likened to the shadow archetype—those aspects of the psyche that are repressed and thus appear monstrous. Their attacks are not random acts of destruction but revelations of hidden truths, confronting humanity with the consequences of its own arrogance. This aligns with Campbell's notion of the "threshold guardian," a force that tests the hero's worthiness and readiness for transformation.

The Angels' ultimate goal, to reunite with Adam and trigger the Third Impact, is a return to cosmic unity—a return that humanity fears, for it entails annihilation. This yearning for unity, however destructive, echoes mystical traditions that seek to dissolve individuality into the divine. In Kabbalistic terms, it is the reunion of the feminine Shekhinah (divine immanence) with the transcendent Ein Sof (infinite light), a process fraught with peril and purification.

### **The Evangelions as New Titans**

The Evangelions themselves carry mythological weight, not merely as tools of war but as beings caught between divine and human realms. They are born of Adam's DNA, making them both children of the divine and products of human manipulation. In this sense, they are akin to the Titans of Greek mythology—beings of immense power but tragic imperfection. The Titans were overthrown by the Olympians, just as the EVAs are destined to fail in their attempt to usurp the Angels' dominion.

Their berserk tendencies, when the pilots lose control, symbolize humanity's failure to master the divine forces it has awakened. These moments of chaos remind us of the Promethean folly inherent in their creation: the attempt to steal fire from the gods always carries the risk of self-destruction.

### **Gendo Ikari as the Demiurge**

Gendo Ikari's role as the manipulative architect of humanity's salvation—or destruction—places him firmly in the tradition of the Gnostic Demiurge. The Demiurge, in Gnostic thought, is a flawed creator who crafts a material world that traps the divine spark within. Gendo's ambition to unite with Yui, his deceased wife and the soul of EVA Unit 01, parallels the Demiurge's misguided attempts to assert dominance over a reality he barely comprehends. His obsession with control, his estrangement from his son Shinji, and his willingness to sacrifice others all highlight the moral and spiritual myopia of his character.

In this framework, Gendo becomes a cautionary tale of hubris: humanity's unrelenting desire to play God without understanding the divine order. His ultimate failure serves as a reminder that creation, when driven by ego and fear, leads not to transcendence but to entropy.

### **The Third Impact as Mythic Catastrophe**

The Third Impact looms as the narrative's apocalyptic crescendo, a cataclysmic event that recalls the flood myths of ancient traditions. However, unlike the biblical flood, which purifies the world in preparation for renewal, the Third Impact threatens a final dissolution—a return to the undifferentiated state before creation. This is both an end and a beginning, mirroring the cyclical destruction and rebirth found in Hindu cosmology.

The Third Impact's promise of unity—a world without individuality, pain, or conflict—tempts and terrifies in equal measure. This temptation echoes the mystical longing for union with the divine, but its realization, as portrayed in _Evangelion_, comes at the cost of the self. Humanity is offered a choice: to dissolve into collective oneness or to endure the alienation and suffering of individuality. This tension embodies the central paradox of the series: the yearning for connection versus the fear of losing oneself.

### **Lilith And Adam: Androgyny and Cosmic Unity**

The fusion of Adam and Lilith in _The End of Evangelion_ represents a return to an androgynous cosmic force, a state that precedes the division of creation into male and female. This merging evokes the Jungian concept of the Self, the integration of all opposing archetypes within the psyche. It also recalls the Gnostic ideal of reunifying the divine masculine and feminine to restore cosmic balance.

In this moment, the Evangelion narrative transcends its apocalyptic trappings to become a myth of individuation. The androgynous union of Adam and Lilith signals not an end but a transformation—a birthing of the cosmos anew. Yet, this act demands a generative protagonist, one who can accept the burden of creation rather than seek refuge in destruction.

## **Section 2: Psychological Depths in _Neon Genesis Evangelion_**

### **The Death Drive and the Allure of Annihilation**

Freud's concept of _Thanatos_, the death drive, finds vivid expression in _Neon Genesis Evangelion_, particularly through the looming specter of the Third Impact. This catastrophic event represents not only physical annihilation but a psychological return to a primal state of pre-conscious unity—a seductive escape from the burdens of individuality. Freud describes _Thanatos_ as the unconscious urge to dissolve the boundaries of the self, and this is mirrored in the series' apocalyptic undertones. For characters like Shinji, the possibility of annihilation offers a paradoxical comfort: freedom from the pain of existence.

This drive is not limited to external destruction; it manifests internally as self-sabotage. Shinji's cycles of avoidance and self-loathing illustrate this dynamic. When overwhelmed by responsibility, he retreats, allowing his fears and insecurities to spiral into destructive withdrawal. In _Evangelion_, the death drive is both personal and collective—a force that threatens humanity's extinction while reflecting the inner turmoil of its characters.

### **The Oedipal Complex and Parental Archetypes**

Freud's _Oedipus complex_ plays out across multiple layers in _Evangelion_. At its heart is the fraught relationship between Shinji and his father, Gendo, who embodies the superego's domineering and judgmental qualities. Gendo is distant and calculating, a figure of authority whose expectations weigh heavily on Shinji. In contrast, the Evangelion units—imbued with the souls of their pilots' mothers—serve as maternal figures. They are both nurturing and terrifying, embodying Freud's notion of the ambivalent mother: at once a source of protection and engulfment.

The EVA's berserk mode, triggered when the pilot loses control, symbolizes the collapse of ego boundaries. In these moments, the maternal id overwhelms the superego, unleashing primal rage. This dynamic captures the psychological tension between autonomy and dependency, a theme that recurs throughout the series.

Shinji's ultimate confrontation with Gendo is not merely a father-son reckoning; it is a confrontation with his own superego, the internalized voice of judgment and inadequacy. The resolution—or lack thereof—of this dynamic becomes central to Shinji's individuation process, which culminates in the series' exploration of the unified and fragmented psyche.

### **Rei Ayanami: Nihilism and the Annihilation of the Self**

Rei Ayanami represents an extreme manifestation of Freud's melancholia. Detached from desire and purpose, she embodies a nihilistic rejection of life itself. Freud describes melancholia as a condition in which the ego becomes absorbed by a sense of worthlessness, and Rei's demeanor reflects this hollow existence. Her disposability—being one of many clones—reinforces her alienation from individuality.

Rei's arc, however, reveals the destructive consequences of denying the self. Her eventual role as the vessel for Lilith's rebirth signifies a transformation from passive nihilism to cosmic generativity. In this shift, Rei transcends her depressive state, becoming a conduit for creation rather than a symbol of despair.

### **The Angels and the Shadow Self**

In Jungian psychology, the shadow represents the repressed aspects of the psyche, those parts of ourselves we fear to acknowledge. The Angels in _Evangelion_ function as externalized shadows, each one confronting humanity with an uncomfortable truth. They are not merely enemies; they are reflections of humanity's unconscious, revealing its fears, desires, and failures.

Each battle with an Angel becomes a psychological trial for the characters. For Shinji, these encounters force him to confront his feelings of inadequacy and his reluctance to engage with the world. The Angels' alien forms and incomprehensible motives emphasize their role as projections of the unknown—the parts of the self that must be integrated rather than defeated.

### **Asuka Langley Soryu: Animus and Ego Defense**

Asuka's fiery confidence and outward aggression mask a fragile ego, a psychological defense mechanism Jung described as animus possession. Her disdain for vulnerability and her tendency to project her weaknesses onto others reveal a profound disconnection from her unconscious. Unlike Shinji, who is overwhelmed by his inner world, Asuka denies hers entirely. This disconnection is symbolized by her EVA's eventual refusal to respond, mirroring her inability to engage with her deeper self.

Asuka's animus-driven persona represents a psychological imbalance. Jung believed that integration of the anima and animus—feminine and masculine aspects of the psyche—was essential for wholeness. Asuka's journey, however, illustrates the consequences of rejecting this integration: alienation, despair, and eventual collapse.

### **Individuation And the Unified Psyche**

At its core, _Neon Genesis Evangelion_ is a narrative of individuation, the Jungian process of integrating the conscious and unconscious to achieve psychic wholeness. Shinji's journey epitomizes this struggle. He must confront not only his shadow but also his anima (as embodied by Rei) and animus (as reflected in Asuka) to move toward a unified Self.

The climactic events of _The End of Evangelion_ present this process on both a personal and cosmic scale. Shinji's final choice—whether to merge with the collective unconscious or to maintain his individuality—represents the ultimate test of individuation. To choose unity is to reject the pain of existence but also the possibility of growth. To choose individuality is to embrace alienation but retain the potential for self-awareness and meaning.

### **The Evas and the Maternal Archetype**

The Evangelions, infused with the souls of their pilots' mothers, embody the Jungian archetype of the Great Mother. They are protectors and destroyers, nurturing yet consuming. This duality mirrors the maternal archetypes of Parvati and Kali in Hindu mythology, reflecting the tension between creation and destruction inherent in the maternal role.

Shinji's reliance on EVA Unit 01, which contains the soul of his mother Yui, underscores his psychological regression. The EVA becomes a literal womb, shielding him from the external world. Yet, this dependency stunts his growth, preventing him from achieving true individuation. The berserk episodes, where the EVA acts without Shinji's control, symbolize the consequences of failing to integrate the maternal archetype into a balanced psyche.

## **Section 3: The Hero's Journey in _Neon Genesis Evangelion_**

### **Departure: The Reluctant Hero**

In Joseph Campbell's _Hero's Journey_, the first stage is departure—a call to adventure that thrusts the hero out of their ordinary world. For Shinji Ikari, this call is not embraced but endured. Summoned to pilot an Evangelion in a world on the brink of annihilation, Shinji is a reluctant hero, embodying resistance rather than courage. His hesitation reflects a deeper psychological truth: the hero's journey begins not with certainty but with fear.

Unlike traditional heroes who answer the call with resolve, Shinji's initial reaction is to flee. This divergence from the archetype underscores the series' deconstruction of heroism. In _Evangelion_, the journey does not glorify the hero's might but exposes their vulnerability. The Evangelion, rather than being a symbol of empowerment, becomes an extension of Shinji's psychological scars, amplifying his inner turmoil.

### **Trials And Ordeals: Confronting the Shadow**

In the _Hero's Journey_, trials serve as tests that prepare the hero for transformation. For Shinji, these trials come in the form of battles against the Angels, each a manifestation of the unknown and the unconscious. The Angels are not merely physical adversaries; they represent the facets of Shinji's psyche he fears most—abandonment, inadequacy, and the weight of responsibility.

These confrontations are also trials of self-worth. Shinji's struggle to pilot the EVA mirrors his internal battle to define himself outside of his father's shadow. His victories are pyrrhic; they bring temporary relief but deepen his existential crisis. The Evangelion, far from being a savior's tool, becomes a crucible where Shinji's fears and failures are magnified.

### **Meeting The Goddess: Rei and the Anima**

In Campbell's framework, the hero encounters a figure who represents the feminine, often guiding the hero toward self-awareness. Rei Ayanami occupies this role as Shinji's anima, the Jungian archetype of the inner feminine. Rei's enigmatic presence forces Shinji to confront his own suppressed emotions and vulnerabilities.

Rei's arc is deeply tied to Shinji's individuation. Her evolution from a passive, nihilistic figure to one of cosmic significance mirrors Shinji's growing awareness of his place in the larger narrative. Her eventual transformation into a vessel for Lilith represents the anima's power to transcend the individual psyche and connect to the collective unconscious.

### **The Abyss: Asuka and the Shadowed Animus**

Asuka Langley Soryu, in contrast, represents the animus—a counterpart to Shinji's anima but laden with unresolved tension. Asuka's fiery persona and unyielding confidence are masks for her own insecurities, creating a distorted mirror for Shinji's struggles. Their relationship becomes a battlefield of projection, with each character projecting their fears and desires onto the other.

Asuka's descent into despair, culminating in her EVA's refusal to respond, symbolizes the hero's encounter with the abyss—a stage in the journey where the hero faces their darkest fears. For Shinji, Asuka's collapse forces him to confront his own failures, highlighting the fragility of his ego and his dependence on others for validation.

### **Atonement With the Father: Gendo as Tyrannical Authority**

A central component of the hero's journey is atonement with the father, a symbolic reconciliation with authority. Gendo Ikari, Shinji's father, represents the tyrannical father archetype—a figure whose judgment looms over the hero's psyche. Gendo's cold, calculating demeanor and his obsession with reuniting with Yui position him as both antagonist and shadow to Shinji's journey.

The final confrontation between Shinji and Gendo is not merely about resolving personal grievances; it is a moment of psychic integration. Gendo embodies the superego's oppressive demands, while Shinji's eventual rejection of his father's worldview signifies his emancipation from external control. This is the hero's atonement—not acceptance of the father's authority, but the transcendence of it.

### **The Return: Unity or Alienation**

The hero's journey culminates in the return, where the hero brings newfound wisdom back to the ordinary world. In _The End of Evangelion_, this return is ambiguous. Shinji's final decision—to reject the collective unity of Instrumentality and embrace individual existence—marks a departure from the traditional hero's arc. Rather than restoring balance to the world, Shinji's choice underscores the series' existentialist ethos: meaning must be forged, not inherited.

This decision reflects the hero's ultimate transformation. Shinji chooses individuality, with all its pain and alienation, over the seductive comfort of unity. In Campbellian terms, Shinji's return is not to a harmonious world but to a fractured one, where the possibility of rebuilding remains.

### **Cosmic Rebirth: the Androgynous Union of Adam and Lilith**

The merging of Adam and Lilith in _The End of Evangelion_ represents a mythic rebirth, aligning with Campbell's motif of the hero's death and resurrection. This cosmic union transcends gender and individuality, symbolizing the androgynous Self that encompasses all opposites. For Shinji, witnessing this event forces him to confront his own generative potential—the ability to create rather than destroy.

This rebirth is not confined to Shinji's personal journey; it reflects a larger existential question posed by the series. Can humanity reconcile its fractured psyche and achieve a state of wholeness, or is it destined to perpetuate its cycles of destruction? _Evangelion_ leaves this question unresolved, challenging the viewer to grapple with its implications.

## **The Mother Soul: Psychic Bonds Beyond Death**

One of _Evangelion_'s most powerful themes is the enduring psychic hold of mothers over their children. The souls of the mothers of Shinji, Asuka, and scientist Akagi are embedded within the EVAs, creating a disturbing dynamic where these mechanical entities both protect their children and threaten to reabsorb them into a metaphorical womb. The EVAs can only be piloted by children whose mothers were sacrificed during the Second Impact, establishing a foundation of trauma that permeates the entire narrative. The suicides of Asuka's and Akagi's mothers add additional layers of unresolved trauma to their relationships with these maternal proxies — the EVA is not merely a weapon but a haunted vessel, a mother that cannot fully die and a child that cannot fully separate.

## **Conclusion: Myth and Psychology in Harmony**

_Neon Genesis Evangelion_ stands as a testament to the power of storytelling to weave together the cosmic and the personal, the mythic and the psychological. Its narrative confronts the deepest questions of existence: What does it mean to be human? How do we reconcile the longing for unity with the fear of losing the self? Through its richly layered characters and symbolism, the series explores these questions with unflinching honesty, leaving viewers with no easy answers.

The mythological underpinnings of the series—Adam and Lilith, the Angels, and the Evangelions themselves—remind us that the stories of creation and destruction have always been our tools for understanding the universe. Yet _Evangelion_ does not merely retell these myths; it reimagines them for a world grappling with existential doubt and technological hubris. In doing so, it asks whether humanity, in its quest for mastery, risks severing its connection to the divine and the unconscious.

On a psychological level, the series delves into the struggles of the individual psyche. Through Shinji, Asuka, and Rei, it explores the pain of alienation, the weight of expectations, and the terror of confronting one's shadow. Drawing on Freud's concepts of the death drive and the Oedipal complex, as well as Jung's archetypes and the process of individuation, _Evangelion_ becomes a mirror of the viewer's own inner conflicts.

In _The End of Evangelion_, Shinji's final decision encapsulates the series' ultimate paradox: the simultaneous longing for connection and the assertion of individuality. By rejecting Instrumentality—the dissolution of all boundaries—he embraces the painful but vital truth of existence: that to be human is to live with uncertainty, to seek meaning in a fractured world. This choice, though fraught with suffering, offers the possibility of growth and renewal.

As a modern myth, _Neon Genesis Evangelion_ transcends its medium. It is not merely a story but an experience—a journey through the labyrinth of the soul, illuminated by the enduring wisdom of mythology and psychology. It challenges us to confront our fears, accept our contradictions, and embrace the complexity of being. In its unresolved tensions, it offers not answers, but the freedom to ask our own questions. This is its lasting gift: an invitation to continue the search for meaning, armed with the courage to face ourselves.
