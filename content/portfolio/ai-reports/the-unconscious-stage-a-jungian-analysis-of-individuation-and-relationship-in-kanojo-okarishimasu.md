## The Unconscious Stage: a Jungian Analysis of Individuation and Relationship in Kanojo Okarishimasu

### Introduction: the Birth of Consciousness in a Rental Relationship

The romantic comedy manga series _Kanojo Okarishimasu_ (_Rent-a-Girlfriend_), penned by Reiji Miyajima, presents on its surface a conventional narrative of youthful romantic entanglement, driven by situational comedy and emotional turmoil. However, a deeper analysis reveals a narrative structure that functions as a profound, if protracted, allegory for the Jungian process of individuation. The series' central premise—a college student renting a girlfriend from a mobile application—is not merely a modern plot device; it is a powerful literalization of the psychological concept of the **Persona**, the social mask an individual presents to the world to make a definite impression while concealing their true nature. The story's persistent conflict, characterised by agonisingly slow progress and cyclical misunderstandings, is a dramatisation of the painful, necessary struggle to evolve from relationships founded upon unconscious projections and societal roles to a state of conscious, individual connection. This journey is encapsulated by Carl Jung's assertion in his 1925 essay, "Marriage as a Psychological Relationship," that "there is no birth of consciousness without pain" , a sentiment that serves as the psychological thesis for the entire manga.

This report will conduct an exhaustive analysis of the deep psychology of _Kanojo Okarishimasu_, employing the theoretical framework of Jungian analytical psychology to deconstruct the behaviours and motivations of its central characters. The analysis will move beyond surface-level plot summary to explore the unconscious dynamics at play, providing a comprehensive understanding of the narrative's psychological architecture and its ultimate trajectory. The primary theoretical lens will be Jung's concepts of the archetypes that structure the human psyche and the relational dynamics they produce. The key concepts forming the analytical toolkit for this report are as follows:

- **The Persona:** This is the social facade, the mask adopted to navigate societal expectations. In the world of the manga, this concept is made tangible through the "perfect girlfriend" identity of Chizuru Mizuhara, a role she performs for clients, which serves to obscure her authentic self, Chizuru Ichinose.
- **The Shadow:** This archetype represents the repressed, unconscious, and often negative aspects of the personality—the "dark side" that the conscious ego refuses to acknowledge. It is vividly embodied in the crippling self-loathing and obsessive tendencies of the protagonist, Kazuya Kinoshita, and the manipulative, destructive nature of his ex-girlfriend, Mami Nanami.
- **The Anima and Animus:** These are the contra-sexual archetypes within the psyche. The **Anima** is the unconscious feminine image within a man, while the **Animus** is the unconscious masculine image within a woman. These archetypes are invariably projected onto others, becoming a primary source of what Jung calls "passionate attraction or aversion".
- **The Container and the Contained:** This is a specific relational dynamic described by Jung, wherein a more complex, multifaceted, and often dissociated personality (the "container") psychologically encompasses a simpler, more unified personality (the "contained"). This dynamic frequently leads to feelings of profound dependence and insecurity in the contained partner and a sense of being trapped or excluded in the container.
- **Individuation:** This is the ultimate goal of psychological development in the Jungian model. It is the lifelong process of integrating the disparate parts of the psyche—conscious and unconscious, Persona and Shadow, ego and Self—to become a whole, differentiated, and authentic individual.

The narrative's central premise of a "rental girlfriend" service is a uniquely effective literary device that externalizes the internal psychological concept of the Persona. It transforms an abstract idea into a tangible, transactional commodity within the story's world. Jung defines the Persona as a "mask, designed on the one hand to make a definite impression upon others, and, on the other, to conceal the true nature of the individual". The service offered in the manga provides precisely this: a pre-packaged, idealized social role that can be procured for a fee. Chizuru's job is, quite literally, to _be_ a Persona for her clients. Kazuya's initial transaction is not an attempt to connect with a genuine person but to _rent a Persona_ in order to project a specific, desirable image to his family and friends after a painful breakup. Consequently, the entire narrative foundation is built upon the literal buying and selling of a social mask. This framework moves the concept of the Persona from a purely internal psychological state to an external, commercialized commodity. The author, whether consciously or not, has thereby created a perfect laboratory to explore the profound psychological consequences of over-identifying with the Persona and the immense difficulty of discovering the "authentic self" that lies behind the carefully constructed, and in this case commercialized, facade.

### The Psyche of Kazuya Kinoshita - a Journey into the Self

The protagonist of _Kanojo Okarishimasu_, Kazuya Kinoshita, is a character who has garnered significant criticism and controversy among the manga's readership. He is frequently labelled as a "trashy MC," a "simp," or a "lustful wretch," with his behaviour characterised by indecisiveness, low self-esteem, and a frustrating lack of development. From a Jungian perspective, however, these widely condemned traits are not merely superficial character flaws. They are a powerful, raw, and unfiltered manifestation of his unintegrated unconscious, particularly his overwhelming Shadow and his projected Anima. His journey is not one of simple self-improvement but a complex and arduous process of psychological integration, a struggle to bring the vast, chaotic contents of his unconscious into a relationship with his conscious ego.

#### Deconstructing the "Trashy MC"

Kazuya's defining characteristics- his crippling self-doubt, obsessive perverted lust, chronic indecisiveness, and compulsive tendency to lie—are a textbook inventory of an unintegrated **Shadow**. Jung describes the Shadow archetype as the repository for all the aspects of the personality that the conscious ego finds unacceptable. It contains "repressed ideas, weaknesses, desires, instincts, and shortcomings" , which is a precise psychological profile of Kazuya.

The intense negative reaction he elicits from many readers stems from the fact that his Shadow is not deeply repressed; it is constantly breaking through and overwhelming his conscious personality. His internal monologues are filled with self-loathing, and his actions are frequently driven by base instincts and profound insecurity.

A prime example of this dynamic is the infamous "heroic BSOD" (Blue Screen of Death) moment, where, in a state of despair, Kazuya has a vividly detailed sexual fantasy of Chizuru being intimate with another man, which results in a physical reaction while he is in a public pool. This scene, widely criticized for resetting his character development, is, from a Jungian standpoint, a moment of psychological possession. Jung posits that when the ego is weakened by shock, confusion, or indecision, the Shadow can overwhelm a person's actions. In that moment, Kazuya is not in conscious control; he is "possessed by his shadow," a state in which he is "always standing in his own light and falling into his own traps". The readers who find him "loathsome" or "pathetic" are reacting to the direct, unfiltered expression of a Shadow that most individuals manage to keep deeply repressed and hidden from public view.

However, the Shadow is not purely negative. Jung emphasized that the Shadow is also a source of vitality, creativity, and instinctual energy. It can contain "a number of good qualities, such as normal instincts, appropriate reactions, and creative impulses". This duality is evident in Kazuya. The same unconscious wellspring that produces his self-loathing and obsessive thoughts also fuels his most admirable traits. His tenacity, his profound loyalty, and his willingness to sacrifice himself for Chizuru—such as when he jumps off a ferry to save her from drowning or dedicates himself to crowdfunding and producing an entire film for her—are not products of his weak, conscious ego. They are powerful, instinctual acts that emerge from the depths of his psyche. These moments of bravery and profound kindness are the positive potential of his Shadow, the "good qualities" that lie dormant beneath the layers of repressed inadequacy.

Therefore, Kazuya's character arc is not about "fixing" his flaws or "curing" his pathetic nature. It is about the arduous process of _integrating_ his Shadow. The path to individuation and psychological wholeness does not involve eradicating the dark side of the personality but rather becoming conscious of it, acknowledging its reality, and bringing it into a balanced relationship with the ego. This is the essence of what is known in analytical psychology as "shadow work". The common critique that Kazuya's character frequently "resets" and that his development is not linear is, in fact, a testament to the psychological realism of his portrayal. The process of integration is never a straight line; it is a difficult, cyclical journey with moments of progress followed by painful regressions. One Reddit user astutely described this as a "swirly or silly straw instead of a straight one". When Kazuya acts with selfless courage, he is successfully channelling the primal, instinctual energy of his Shadow. When he despairs and regresses into obsessive fantasy, he is being consumed by its negative, destructive aspects. The entire narrative can thus be understood as a dramatization of this internal struggle. His eventual psychological "success" will not be the eradication of his insecure or perverted thoughts, but the development of a strong enough ego to act as a conscious individual _despite_ them. This reframes the perception of his character from that of a "failed protagonist" to a deeply human and relatable individual undergoing a profoundly difficult, yet universal, psychological process.

#### 1.2 The Anima Projection: Chizuru as a Divine Image

Kazuya's intense and unwavering fixation on Chizuru Mizuhara is a textbook example of a powerful **Anima projection**. Jung posits that every man carries within his unconscious an "eternal image of woman," an archetype he termed the Anima. This image is "fundamentally unconscious, an hereditary factor of primordial origin," and it is "always unconsciously projected upon the person of the beloved," serving as "one of the chief reasons for passionate attraction or aversion". Kazuya does not fall in love with Chizuru Ichinose, the complex and flawed human being. Instead, he is seized by what Jung calls a "fatal compulsion" for Chizuru Mizuhara, the idealized Persona she performs. His attraction is not a conscious choice but an archetypal event; he has found a living vessel for his inner feminine ideal.

This projection is defined by its "erotic, emotional character" and its tendency to deify the object of affection. Kazuya consistently perceives Chizuru not as an equal partner but as a "prize to be won" , an untouchable goddess, a perfect being far beyond his reach. This is why his internal monologues are filled with worshipful reverence and why he feels fundamentally unworthy of her. The Anima projection is so potent that it drives him to perform monumental, almost mythic, feats. The most significant of these is his decision to crowdfund, produce, and direct an entire feature film to fulfill her dream of being seen by her ailing grandmother. This extraordinary act, while framed as being for Chizuru, is psychologically an act of devotion to the Anima image he has projected onto her. He is building a temple for his goddess, creating a _femme inspiratrice_ in the Jungian sense—an inspiring muse whose archetypal power can lead a man to achieve great things.

The central romantic tension of the manga is therefore not a simple "will they/won't they" dynamic. It is a direct psychological conflict between Kazuya's projected Anima and the reality of Chizuru's authentic, complex, and flawed self. The narrative cannot reach a stable resolution until Kazuya successfully withdraws this projection and learns to see, and love, the actual woman behind the archetypal image. Jung explicitly warns that taking such a projection for a "conscious relationship" is a "failure" and an "illusion with destructive consequences". For hundreds of chapters, Kazuya's entire relational world is built upon this very illusion.

Chizuru's persistent emotional distance, her mixed signals, and her often cold demeanor can be understood, in part, as an unconscious reaction to the impossible standard of the Anima being projected onto her. She cannot possibly live up to the flawless "perfect girlfriend" image that he holds in his mind. Kazuya's moments of intense despair, such as when he stalks her on what he perceives to be a date with another man, Umi , are crises of the projection. He is not witnessing a person meeting a friend; in his unconscious, he is witnessing his divine image "betraying" him, which shatters his idealized world. The process of individuation for Kazuya fundamentally requires him to differentiate between the archetype within his own psyche and the human being who exists externally. The recent narrative developments, as of the hypothetical Chapter 390, where Kazuya begins to feel genuine frustration and to criticize Chizuru's actions , are the first, painful, but absolutely necessary steps in this differentiation. He is beginning to see the flaws in the goddess, which is the prerequisite for finally being able to see the human being. The true climax of his emotional journey will not be a simple confession of love, but the moment he can love _Chizuru Ichinose_, the real person, rather than simply worshiping the projected image of _Chizuru Mizuhara_.

#### 1.3 The Influence of the Matriarch: Nagomi and the Parental Complex

Kazuya's psychological immaturity and cripplingly low self-esteem are not solely products of his own making; they are deeply rooted in an unconscious tie to a parental figure, specifically his grandmother, Nagomi Kinoshita. Jung emphasized that "the relationship of the young man to his mother, and of the girl to her father, is the determining factor" in the unconscious motivations that shape adult relationships and mate selection. In the Kinoshita family, Nagomi functions as the undisputed matriarchal authority, a powerful figure whose approval Kazuya desperately craves and rarely receives.

Nagomi's consistent belittling of Kazuya, her overt disappointment in him, and her readiness to label him a "no-good loser" have had a devastating impact on his psychic development. This constant negative reinforcement from a primary attachment figure has crippled his ego and fostered the growth of his powerful, self-loathing Shadow. Her immediate and overwhelming approval of Chizuru—the perfect, polite, beautiful, and successful woman—creates an intense pressure on Kazuya to maintain the elaborate lie of their relationship. He becomes trapped in a relationship not with a person, but with a Persona that satisfies his grandmother's ideals. This dynamic perfectly aligns with Jung's observation that a son with a strong, unconscious parental tie may be compelled to choose a partner in a way that is "contrary to his true nature" in order to resolve this complex. In Kazuya's case, he clings to a partner who perfectly embodies his grandmother's values, thereby seeking her approval and validation through his choice of a mate.

Nagomi is, therefore, the primary architect of Kazuya's psychological prison. Her love is starkly conditional; it is granted only when Kazuya conforms to her collective, traditional expectations, such as having a "proper" girlfriend and preparing to inherit the family liquor store. This dynamic actively prevents his individuation. By reinforcing his identification with a false Persona (the successful boyfriend of the perfect Chizuru) and simultaneously deepening his Shadow's feelings of inadequacy (since he knows he is a fraud), she keeps him in a state of psychological fragmentation. Jung explains that children are often unconsciously driven to "compensate for everything that was left unfulfilled in the lives of their parents". Nagomi's obsession with Kazuya's romantic and professional success is a projection of her own collective values onto him. His life must validate her worldview.

This reframes the entire central deception of the manga. Kazuya's desperation to maintain the lie about Chizuru is not driven solely by his romantic or lustful feelings for her. It is, on a much deeper level, fueled by his desperate, pathological need for his grandmother's approval—an approval he has been starved of his entire life. The lie is not just a romantic comedy trope; it is a symptom of a deep-seated parental complex. He is not simply trying to win a girlfriend; he is unconsciously trying to heal a core wound inflicted by his family dynamic. Consequently, Nagomi is not a simple comedic side character who provides occasional comic relief. From a psychological perspective, she is a key antagonist to Kazuya's development. His individuation requires him to break free not just from his obsessive projection onto Chizuru, but, more fundamentally, from his pathological need for his grandmother's validation. He must learn to find his self-worth within himself, independent of her judgment, before he can ever hope to form a healthy, conscious relationship with another person.

### Part Ii: the Women in Kazuya's Orbit - Archetypes and Individuals

The narrative of _Kanojo Okarishimasu_ is propelled by the complex and often fraught interactions between Kazuya and the primary women in his life. Each of these female characters—Chizuru Mizuhara, Mami Nanami, and Nagomi Kinoshita—can be understood not merely as a romantic interest, antagonist, or family member, but as a living embodiment of powerful Jungian archetypes. They function as externalizations of the psychological forces that Kazuya must confront on his path to individuation. Chizuru represents the tyranny of the Persona, Mami the destructive power of the unintegrated Shadow, and Nagomi the overwhelming influence of the Great Mother and the collective. Their psychologies are as central to the story's deep structure as Kazuya's own.

#### 2.1 Chizuru Mizuhara and the Tyranny of the Persona

Chizuru's character provides a masterful exploration of the Jungian **Persona**. Her entire professional life is built upon the meticulous construction and performance of a social mask. The "Chizuru Mizuhara" identity is not a person but a product: the "perfect girlfriend," designed to be charming, supportive, and beautiful, thereby making a "definite impression upon others". This Persona is so well-developed and successful that it has become a psychological cage, concealing her true self: the "dorky, anti-social, and secretive drama geek" known as Chizuru Ichinose. Jung warned of the profound danger that arises when individuals "become identical with their personas," a state that can lead to a "shallow, brittle, conformist kind of personality" where the authentic self is smothered.

Chizuru's primary internal conflict stems from this over-identification. Her chronic inability to communicate her true feelings, her repeated pattern of "ghosting" Kazuya after moments of genuine emotional intimacy, and her constant retreat into the "safe" excuse of her acting career are all symptoms of a psyche terrified of letting the mask fall. When confronted with a genuine emotional choice, she defaults to the logic of her profession. This is where her **Animus**, the unconscious masculine aspect of her psyche, comes into play. Jung describes the Animus as having a "rationalizing" character, one that can produce an "inexhaustible supply of illogical arguments and false explanations" to protect the ego from difficult emotional truths. Chizuru's Animus manifests as her relentless, single-minded drive for her acting career. This ambition, while admirable, also functions as her primary defense mechanism. It provides her with a constant, "logical" reason to maintain emotional distance and delay any real confrontation with her feelings for Kazuya, as seen in her prioritization of her July 11th play above all else.

The core of Chizuru's psychological struggle is the profound fear of the **disintegration of the Persona**. To confess her feelings to Kazuya, or to enter into a real relationship with him, would mean shattering the "Mizuhara" mask and revealing the vulnerable, insecure "Ichinose" self. This is a self she unconsciously deems unworthy of love, a belief likely rooted in deep-seated trauma such as the abandonment by her father in her childhood, which can lead to overwhelming fears of abandonment and a sense of low self-worth in adult relationships. Her Persona is not just a professional tool; it is a shield, a carefully constructed fortress designed to protect her from the possibility of being seen and then rejected.

The "Mizuhara" persona is controlled, competent, and universally desired. The "Ichinose" self, in her own perception, is flawed, insecure, and fears being a burden. Every time Kazuya breaks through her defenses and forces a moment of authentic connection, she is faced with the terrifying choice to let this mask drop. Her repeated rejections and evasions are not necessarily a sign of a lack of feeling, but rather a panicked retreat from this moment of potential psychological annihilation. The narrative arc following the kiss in chapter 387 is the peak of this conflict. The kiss itself was a rare, impulsive, and authentic act from Ichinose. The subsequent rejection, ghosting, and deflection onto her work were the panicked reactions of her ego, retreating into the safety of the Mizuhara Persona and the rationalizations provided by her Animus. Her path to individuation requires her to find the courage to endure this disintegration, to let the mask break, and to discover that her true, authentic self is worthy of love and connection.

#### 2.2 Mami Nanami as the Embodiment of the Collective Shadow

Mami Nanami, Kazuya's ex-girlfriend, functions within the narrative as more than a simple romantic antagonist or "villain." From a Jungian perspective, she is a personification of the **collective Shadow**, the dark, repressed, and destructive potential that exists within the human psyche. While Kazuya's Shadow is largely personal and introverted, manifesting as self-loathing and internal turmoil, Mami's is externalized, projected, and actively destructive. Her defining character traits—described as "sociopathic tendencies, such as conniving, manipulative, and petty behaviors"—are classic manifestations of an unintegrated and hostile Shadow.

Mami operates by projecting her own unacknowledged inner darkness onto the world around her. She is driven by a cynical belief that all love is "make-believe" and seeks to expose the fraudulent nature of Kazuya and Chizuru's relationship. This compulsion stems from her own profound trauma: her capacity for genuine connection was systematically destroyed by her controlling parents, who sabotaged a relationship she genuinely valued in favor of an arranged marriage. This formative experience created a deep wound that she has never processed. Instead of integrating this pain, she has repressed it, allowing it to fester into a cynical, destructive Shadow that cannot tolerate the sight of genuine affection in others.

Like Chizuru, Mami wears a highly effective **Persona**—a sweet, friendly, angelic facade that masterfully conceals the seething rage and jealousy of her Shadow. In archetypal terms, she embodies the **Trickster**. The Trickster is a figure who operates through chaos, deception, and disruption. While their actions are often malicious, they serve a crucial psychological function: they shatter illusions and force the protagonists to confront uncomfortable truths. Mami's relentless attempts to expose the rental arrangement, while motivated by spite, are the primary force that pushes the narrative toward a necessary confrontation with the fundamental lie upon which Kazuya and Chizuru's relationship is built. She is the agent of a painful, but necessary, reality check.

Mami's character serves as a powerful cautionary tale of failed individuation. Trapped by a severe parental complex and completely possessed by her unintegrated Shadow, she is unconsciously compelled to attack any semblance of a genuine connection she observes in others. This is because the possibility of authentic love in the world is a direct and painful reminder of her own profound psychological lack. She is the dark mirror to both Kazuya and Chizuru. She sees in Kazuya's unwavering devotion to Chizuru the very thing she was forced to give up, and her Shadow cannot tolerate its existence. Her antagonism is not simple jealousy over losing a boyfriend she didn't even want; it is the desperate, destructive act of an unindividuated psyche lashing out at the possibility of a psychological wholeness that she was denied. By trying to prove _their_ love is fake, she is unconsciously attempting to validate her own cynical worldview and justify her own emotional emptiness.

#### 2.3 Nagomi Kinoshita and the Great Mother Archetype

Grandma Nagomi Kinoshita is a pivotal figure in the narrative, embodying the powerful and dualistic **Great Mother** archetype. This archetype, central to Jungian thought, possesses two opposing aspects: the nurturing, life-giving mother and the devouring, consuming mother. Nagomi exhibits both sides in her relationship with Kazuya. On one hand, she is the nurturing matriarch, deeply invested in her grandson's future happiness and the continuation of the family line. Her joy upon meeting Chizuru is genuine, stemming from a sincere desire to see Kazuya find a worthy partner.

On the other hand, she is the devouring mother, whose rigid expectations and constant judgments stifle Kazuya's psychological development and autonomy. Her love is profoundly conditional, contingent upon his adherence to a narrow set of collective, traditional norms: he must secure a "perfect" girlfriend like Chizuru and eventually take over the family business. This immense pressure forces Kazuya into a state of what Jung might call "neurotic… alienation from a deeper sense of oneself". He is so focused on meeting her external standards that he loses touch with his own internal reality. By idealizing Chizuru's Persona, Nagomi inadvertently becomes the single greatest obstacle to Kazuya forming a genuine relationship with Chizuru's true self.

In this role, Nagomi represents the overwhelming power of the **collective consciousness**. She is the voice of society, tradition, and family expectation. The process of individuation requires an individual to differentiate themselves from these collective pressures, to find their own unique path rather than simply living out a pre-ordained script. Nagomi's constant presence and judgment make this differentiation incredibly difficult for Kazuya.

The deep and immediate friendship that forms between Nagomi and Chizuru's grandmother, Sayuri, is symbolically significant. This bond is a powerful representation of the collective's desire for the union of Kazuya and Chizuru's _Personas_, not their authentic selves. It is the older generation imposing a conventional, archetypal narrative of a "good match" onto the younger generation. Their relationship is founded on mutual illusions: Nagomi is enamored with the "Mizuhara" Persona, while Sayuri sees Kazuya through the lens of the devoted, perfect boyfriend he is only pretending to be.

This friendship between the grandmothers, while heartwarming on the surface, functions as a gilded psychological cage for the protagonists. It acts as a powerful narrative force that locks them into their false roles. To reveal the truth would be to disappoint these beloved matriarchs, a betrayal of a deeply held collective value. Jung emphasizes that individuation requires divesting the self from the "false wrappings of the persona" and resisting the "suggestive power of primordial images"—that is, collective expectations. Therefore, the grandmothers' bond is a formidable obstacle on this path. The death of Sayuri was a crucial, albeit tragic, narrative event that freed Chizuru from one half of this collective pressure, forcing her to pursue her dreams for her own sake. Similarly, Kazuya's eventual, complete individuation will require him to find the strength to disappoint Nagomi's idealized vision in order to establish an authentic life and relationship for himself.

### Part Iii: the Relational Dynamics - a Jungian Analysis of Connection

The relationship between Kazuya and Chizuru is the engine of the _Kanojo Okarishimasu_ narrative, yet it is defined by stasis, miscommunication, and a profound lack of progress. From a Jungian perspective, this is not a flaw in the storytelling but rather a psychologically astute depiction of a relationship that has not yet achieved a conscious, individual state. Their dynamic is governed by specific unconscious patterns—namely, the "container and the contained" dynamic and the pervasive influence of mutual projection—which create a state of psychological deadlock. For the majority of the series, they do not have what Jung would define as a true "psychological relationship" at all.

#### The Container and the Contained Dynamic

The Kazuya-Chizuru relationship is a perfect illustration of Jung's "container" and "contained" dynamic, a concept he outlines to explain a common source of marital crisis. In this model, one partner, the **container**, possesses a more complex, multifaceted, and often dissociated nature. The other partner, the **contained**, is simpler and more emotionally unified in their focus on the relationship.

Chizuru is the quintessential **container**. Her personality is fragmented into multiple, often conflicting, parts: the hidden, insecure "Ichinose" self; the professional, perfect "Mizuhara" persona; and the ambitious, driven actress. She is a "many-sided and rather problematical nature" who, in Jung's words, gives her simpler partner "too many rooms with too much space, so that she never knows where she really belongs". Kazuya is constantly lost in the labyrinth of her psyche, unable to grasp her true nature because she herself has not integrated its disparate parts.

Kazuya, conversely, is the **contained**. His focus on Chizuru is total and "undivided." He feels that he is "living entirely within the confines" of their pseudo-relationship, which leads to a state of "disquieting dependence upon a personality that can never be seen in its entirety, and is therefore not altogether credible or dependable". This precisely describes Kazuya's psychological state: his entire sense of self-worth becomes dependent on the unpredictable and opaque actions of Chizuru.

This dynamic creates a toxic feedback loop that explains the core frustration of the series. As Jung describes, "The more the contained clings, the more the container feels shut out of the relationship". Kazuya's desperate, clinging attempts to get closer and secure her affection only serve to heighten Chizuru's feeling of being trapped and misunderstood, causing her to withdraw further into the "rooms" of her own psyche—her work, her secrets, her rationalizations. Kazuya's clinging (born of insecurity) triggers Chizuru's withdrawal (born of fear and complexity), which in turn deepens Kazuya's insecurity, leading to even more desperate clinging.

The manga's infamous and often-criticized slow pacing is a direct narrative consequence of this psychological deadlock. The stasis is not a storytelling flaw but a realistic depiction of this specific relational trap. The lack of forward momentum _is_ the plot. It is a story about being psychologically stuck. Jung predicts that the only way to break this cycle is through a crisis that forces a fundamental shift in both partners. The crisis must compel the contained (Kazuya) to stop seeking security in the other and instead find it within himself. Simultaneously, it must force the container (Chizuru) to stop seeking completion externally (e.g., in her career) and instead turn inward to confront and integrate her own complexities. The events following the kiss and subsequent rejection in the recent chapters represent precisely this critical, transformative crisis. The old dynamic has been shattered, and a new, more conscious one must be born from its ashes.

#### 3.2 Projection and the Failure of Psychological Relationship

According to Jung's rigorous definition, a true "psychological relationship" is a conscious phenomenon. It can only exist where there is a degree of consciousness and the ability for two individuals to differentiate themselves from one another. He states unequivocally, "there is no such thing as a psychological relationship between two people who are in a state of unconsciousness". Where large areas of the psyche remain unconscious, any relationship is limited and incomplete.

Judged by this standard, for the vast majority of the manga's run, Kazuya and Chizuru do not have a psychological relationship. They exist in a state of "partial unconsciousness," their interactions governed almost entirely by powerful, mutual, and unacknowledged projections. A relationship based on projection is not a connection between two real people; it is a drama enacted by archetypes.

Kazuya's side of this dynamic is dominated by the projection of his **Anima** onto Chizuru. He is not interacting with her, but with the idealized feminine image from within his own unconscious. Chizuru, for her part, interacts with Kazuya through the filter of her **Persona**. She is not her authentic self, but the role she is paid to play. She projects onto him the role of the "problematic client" or, later, the "supportive but not-quite-real boyfriend," a perception shaped and justified by the rationalizations of her **Animus**. They are not two individuals communicating; they are a collection of archetypes and psychological complexes interacting on a stage of their own making. This is the fundamental reason why their communication is so profoundly and consistently broken. They are speaking different psychological languages, one from the emotional, idealized world of the Anima, the other from the detached, role-playing world of the Persona and the rationalizing Animus.

The financial transaction of "renting" is a brilliant and literal metaphor for the psychological cost of maintaining these projections. The relationship is transactional precisely because it is not yet authentically relational. Kazuya literally pays money to keep his Anima projection alive; his payments are a tribute offered to the goddess he perceives her to be. Chizuru, by accepting the payment, enters into a contract to uphold her Persona, to perform the role he needs her to play. This commercial framework institutionalizes the inauthenticity. It creates a power imbalance and reinforces their assigned roles, making the formation of a genuine, conscious bond nearly impossible.

The moment the financial arrangement becomes irrelevant—as it largely has in the later stages of the story—the psychological scaffolding that supports these projections begins to collapse. This forces a crisis. The old rules of client and provider, of worshipper and goddess, no longer apply. They are left in a void, forced to confront the lack of a real, conscious relationship underneath the layers of performance and projection. Their current state of confusion and emotional distance is the direct result of this collapse. They must now learn to relate to one another as individuals, without the mediating structure of the rental contract or the distorting lens of archetypal projection.

### The Trajectory towards Individuation (Post-Chapter 390)

As of the hypothetical narrative point of Chapter 390 in August 2025, _Kanojo Okarishimasu_ has reached the critical juncture that Carl Jung describes as the "necessary prelude to conscious realization". The central relationship, long defined by unconscious projection and the rigid performance of social roles, has been shattered by a moment of authentic, yet unresolved, intimacy. Chizuru's impulsive kiss, followed immediately by her rejection of Kazuya, her subsequent ghosting, and her retreat into the "safe" excuse of her upcoming play, represents the zenith of her Persona's defensive strategy. She is desperately attempting what Jung termed a "regressive restoration of the persona"—a frantic effort to patch up her social mask and pretend that the crucial, mask-shattering experience never happened, hoping to return to the controlled safety of their previous dynamic.

Simultaneously, Kazuya is exhibiting the first, painful signs of genuine psychological differentiation. His newfound willingness to feel and express frustration with Chizuru's behavior, and to criticize her actions rather than simply accepting them with passive adoration, indicates that his powerful Anima projection is beginning to crack. He is no longer just a worshipper at the feet of a goddess; he is an individual who is being emotionally wounded by another individual's actions. This dawning awareness of himself as a separate being with legitimate feelings is a crucial step toward withdrawing the projection and seeing Chizuru not as an archetype, but as a person.

Given this psychological turning point, the manga's ultimate endgame is not a simple romantic confession but the arduous, dual process of **individuation** for both protagonists. The narrative's resolution requires them to become whole individuals before they can form a whole relationship.

For Chizuru, the path forward necessitates the "disintegration" of her Mizuhara Persona, a process Jung describes as terrifying but essential for growth. The July 11th stage play is not, as she claims, the primary obstacle to their relationship; it is the symbolic stage upon which this psychological drama will likely culminate. The performance will likely serve as a crucible, forcing her to confront the emptiness of a life lived through a mask and the profound reality of her repressed feelings for Kazuya. The narrative will likely engineer a crisis during or after the play that leaves her with no choice but to let the Persona fall, allowing the authentic, vulnerable "Ichinose" self to finally emerge and seek a genuine connection.

For Kazuya, the journey requires the full withdrawal of his Anima projection and, most importantly, the integration of his Shadow. This means he must find a stable sense of self-worth that is independent of Chizuru's validation and untethered from his lifelong quest for Nagomi's approval. He must consciously acknowledge and accept the "trashy" parts of himself—his insecurities, his obsessive tendencies—not as sources of shame to be hidden, but as integral parts of his total personality. This integration will transform him from the dependent "contained" partner into a whole, separate individual, capable of meeting Chizuru as an equal. His journey is to realize that he is worthy of love not as a "loser" who got lucky, but as a complete, albeit flawed, human being.

The final resolution of _Kanojo Okarishimasu_ will be the moment Kazuya and Chizuru can finally establish a true **psychological relationship**, as defined by Jung: one based on consciousness, differentiation, and mutual recognition of each other's authentic individuality. This can only occur after they have each embarked on the solitary path of becoming individuals in their own right. The manga's ultimate message, when viewed through this sophisticated psychological lens, is a powerful one. It suggests that true, mature love is not the ecstatic, unconscious fusion of two incomplete halves into "one heart and one soul"—a state Jung identifies with primitive, collective relationships. Rather, it is the conscious, respectful, and loving connection that can only be formed between two whole, individuated selves. The narrative's long, frustrating, and arduous path is not a weakness but its greatest strength, serving as a testament to the profound, and often painful, difficulty of that essential human journey.
