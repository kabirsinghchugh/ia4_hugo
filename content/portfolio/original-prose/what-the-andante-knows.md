### What the *Andante* Knows

I have been listening to the second movement of Mozart's *Piano Concerto No. 21* on and off for about a decade, and I am only now beginning to understand why I cannot stop. The piece is not beautiful in the obvious way. It is beautiful in the way that a koan is beautiful, which is to say that it withholds something from you, and the withholding is the point.

What it withholds, I think, is consolation.

There is a common misreading of this *Andante* that treats it as a sad piece of music, a lament dressed in courtly clothes. This is wrong. The piece has an elegiac surface, yes; it is wistful, mournful, reflective. And yet underneath the long sighing line of the strings runs a curious bouncing pulse, three repeated notes in the accompaniment that refuse to mourn properly. They hop. They almost wink. They give the whole movement the quality of a man weeping at his own funeral and finding the situation a little ridiculous.

This refusal to settle is what the piece is *about*. So let me stake a position. The *Andante* is a philosophical proposition, made in sound, about the relationship between **Tragedy** and **Comedy**. And the proposition is roughly this: they are the same fact, observed from two different distances.

Consider what happens when you stand close to grief. Your own grief, say. It is total. It fills the available space. There is no room for irony because irony requires a vantage point, and grief annihilates vantage points. This is the condition of the protagonist in classical Greek tragedy, who cannot see the absurdity of his situation because he is *in* it. Now step back. Far enough back, and the same situation begins to acquire a strange comic geometry. The man weeping at the funeral, the bishop kidnapped from his cathedral, the wanderer who has lost everything and is still putting one foot in front of the other; observed from sufficient distance, every tragedy contains a punchline. This is parallax, not cruelty.

The *Andante* somehow holds both distances at once. The piano line gives you the close view, the trembling intimacy of someone undone. The three hopping notes give you the far view, the bird's-eye absurdity of the same scene. Mozart does not alternate between these perspectives. He superimposes them. And the result is a sensation almost unique in Western music: you are weeping and you are also amused that you are weeping, and these two states sit together without conflict.

How does he sustain this? By a method I can only describe as choreographed dissociation.

There is a figure who walks through this piece, though Mozart never names him. Call him the wanderer. He moves through some small town at evening, watching the ordinary tragicomedy of life unfold around him. A child runs after a dog. An old woman closes her shutters. A man stands in a doorway looking at nothing. The wanderer sees these things, and because he is at a slight remove from his own life, he sees them clearly. The world moves; he stands still. And every so often, between observations, something flickers in him. A small private opening. He becomes momentarily aware that he too is being observed, that he is also a figure in someone's evening, also a small comic-tragic shape moving across someone else's window. And then the awareness closes again and he walks on.

This pattern of opening and closing is the shape of the entire movement.

The piece descends, by stages, into deeper and deeper interiority. By the time the flute enters, the wanderer is no longer watching the town. He is watching himself, and the watching has become unbearable. He arrives at some recognition about his own life, the kind of recognition that one cannot quite put into language because language softens it, and what is required here is precisely the absence of softening. The piano slows. The high notes arrive with the quality of breath held too long. There is a silence inside the music that is somehow louder than the notes. This is the centre of the piece, its black sun, and Mozart will not let you stay there long.

The return is what I find almost unbearable.

Around the six-and-a-half-minute mark the music begins to climb back toward the surface. The wanderer is leaving the moment of clarity and going back to ordinary life, the life of paying for the bread and remembering to lock the door. He is going back to what one might call *multifarious living*: the comfortable, repetitive, slightly absurd texture of daily existence. And here Mozart performs his cruellest and kindest stroke. He lets the wanderer want to return. The mask of ordinary life is welcome; the wanderer is tired of seeing himself naked. And yet he is also bringing something back with him, some residue of what he saw, which will sit in him for the rest of his life and give a slight sadness to even his happy moments.

This is the human condition, I assert, more accurately rendered than in almost any philosophy I have read. Kierkegaard tried to describe it, in his categories of dread and despair. Bergson approached it from the side, through his account of duration. The *Tractatus* gestures at it, and then refuses to say more, because what cannot be said cannot be said. Mozart simply plays it.

Why does the piece work? Because it does not lie about the asymmetry.

Most music about suffering is consolatory. It assures you that the suffering meant something, that the descent had a purpose, that the resolution was earned. The *Andante* offers no such assurance. The descent is real. The clarity is real. The return to ordinary life is real. None of these cancels the others. You go down, you see something true, you come back up, and the world is exactly as it was, and you are not exactly as you were, and there is no further moral. The piece refuses to flatter the listener with the suggestion that authentic experience is sustainable, or that having had it makes you better, or that the mask of daily life is a betrayal. The mask is necessary. The brief unmasking is also necessary. Both are true at once.

And this, finally, is where the comedy lives. Not in any specific figure or gesture, but in the structural fact that human beings are creatures who can briefly see themselves clearly and then must, by biological necessity, go back to not seeing themselves clearly. We are designed for partial vision. The *Andante* knows this and forgives us for it.

So when the last notes fade, what remains is something subtler and harder to name than catharsis. A kind of tender amusement at the predicament of being human at all. The wanderer walks on. The town continues. The bread still needs paying for. And somewhere in the listener, something the music has shown is now permanent.

That is what the *Andante* knows. It knows that we cannot stay, and that we cannot quite forget either, and that the small overlap between these two facts is everything.
