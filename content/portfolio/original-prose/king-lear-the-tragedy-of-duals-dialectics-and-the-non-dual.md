## The Tragedy of King Lear

King Lear is my favourite tragedy from the repertoire of Shakespeare. For me, it stands at the highest levels of true profundity alongside Sophocles' _Oedipus Rex_ and Euripides' _Medea_. What differentiates Shakespeare from the great Greek tragedians is that while the Greeks reached the Sublime in the overall story and inherent symbolism notwithstanding the performances as a whole, Shakespeare's genius is not in the plot itself, but the masterful word play and the capture of Sublime within the dialogues , which couldn't be said of the other two. It is commonly known that Shakespeare took archetypal stories and transformed them, infusing them with words to transcend all time.

The plot in brief is thus - _The old and increasingly loony King Lear decides to retire from kingly affairs and carves up his kingdom to his three daughters, two of which - Goneril and Regan, are sycophants and shower him with empty words while the third, Cordelia, who loves him the most, refuses to use words as cheap jewellery. The enraged King then disowns her, and the King of France agrees to marry her, impressed by her courage and honesty. The kingdom is then divided to the two daughters. Lear also exiles his faithful servant, the Earl of Kent, for questioning the actions of the king towards Cordelia, and hence the King disposes of his powers to sycophants. This leads to the tragedy, where the king's daughters reveal their true nature and humiliate the once powerful king to strip him of his dignity to leave him as just an old, withering fool. Insulted at both his daughters' treatment, the king loses his sanity completely, accompanied by just his jester( The Fool) in a ravaging storm, where one of the most striking passages of the play take place. Ultimately, the kings' loyal followers unite and wage a war against the daughters' armies, with tragic consequences for everyone.

### Analysis

There are whole books devoted to the themes in King Lear, but I shall sketch out the ones which struck me during my reading.

#### Duality and Conflict

The whole play can be thought of as the opposition of dualities and their resolution, _a la_ **Hegelian Dialectic**. It is the contrast between Good and Evil, Idealism and Pragmatism, Duty and Self Interest, Reality and Illusion, Reason and Irrationality and overarchingly the collateral damage inherent in creative destruction - the Old and the New. It is an interesting coincidence that the environment in which Shakespeare was alive was a period where Medieval Europe desperately tried to hold on to its culture, even patronisingly enforcing it upon the masses, with the nascent wave of the oncoming Renaissance, Atheism, Rationality and Revolutions. This play(for me at least) is the clash of the two, with Shakespeare revolving it through a harsh commentary upon both sides, with the tragedy being an answer to which is better, the answer clearly being none. The play hence can be thought of in two perspectives, of the two different sides, with the third perspective of one of the most enduring characters, the fool, who in his comedic dialogues is the third perspective, with the harshest witticisms yet insignificant, ignored by all the characters except us, the readers.

King Lear represents the archetypal pompous individual, whose requirement of shallow praises deludes him from the reality, and ends up losing everything only to be discarded to show what he really is - An old withering dying fool, which upon realising goes mad. But only in his madness, he sees the world for what it is, which gobbles up the once invincible into decomposition. His lack of this realisation is akin to medieval europe's aristocracy, desperately clinging to earlier glory and showers of praises, deaf to the sound of the guillotine blade sharpening in the background.

### Are We All King Lears?

A major theme in _King Lear_, perhaps the most profound among all Shakespeare's plays, is that of _Madness_.

> If the World itself is Insane. Then the sane are the mad; the mad are the sane.

King Lear is the revered King, who in actuality is the pompous fool, deluded to not see Reality for what it is. Most of us are _King Lears_ of the world — we cling to our illusory selves, see ourselves as the virtuous. We don't see that the veneer of invincibility we feel is but a cloak for the inevitable, ongoing decomposition within.

### Shakespeare as the Fool

Perhaps Shakespeare himself has donned the clothes of the Fool, so that in the real world, he could escape the consequences despite making his point, lest the Nobles of his time actually manage to peel the layers off and find that indeed, our real world is nothing but a larger _King Lear_ in action.

The Fool's own predicament and helplessness may be an echo of Shakespeare's own helplessness.

### The Eternal Struggle

Shakespeare was prescient in looking at the overall scheme of things with a degree of pessimism and skepticism rather than the naive optimism of many progressive thinkers of his time, yet he wasn't a conservative either. The struggle is an eternal one — in future this morphed into **Rationalism versus Romanticism, Romanticism vs Modernism, and finally Modernism versus Post-Modernism.** Just as in the play, Shakespeare's harsh commentary reverberates even today, with his answer clearly echoing **Neither!**

### Key Quotes Not in the Main Draft

The Fool's most cutting line to Lear:

> "Thou shouldst not have been old till thou hadst been wise."

This encapsulates one of the play's central ironies: Lear's lack of self-awareness and wisdom, despite his advanced age.

Lear's existential cry — his most nihilistic:

> "As flies to wanton boys are we to th' gods; / They kill us for their sport."

The final scene — Lear cradling Cordelia's body:

> "Howl, howl, howl! O, you are men of stones."

His cry reflects anguish not only at the loss of his daughter but at the realisation of his own role in the tragedy. Lear's journey from prideful king to broken man is complete; in his suffering, he gains a tragic wisdom.

### Cordelia Reconsidered

Cordelia, often seen as the play's moral centre, embodies the ideal of truthfulness and loyalty. Yet, even she is not spared. Her refusal to flatter her father may be rooted in honesty, but it is also a form of stubbornness that ultimately costs her life. Shakespeare's portrayal complicates the notion of virtue: even the most noble intentions can lead to ruin in a world governed by pride and deception.

### Edgar: Madness as Liberation

Edgar's journey contrasts with Lear's. In adopting the guise of Poor Tom, Edgar gains insight into the human condition and ultimately becomes a force of redemption. His path suggests that madness can be both a descent **and** a means of renewal — where Lear's madness destroys, Edgar's performed madness preserves.

The Fool's ultimate disappearance can be interpreted as a reflection of Lear's growing self-awareness. Once Lear fully acknowledges his own folly, the Fool's role is no longer necessary.

### Lear's Power Paradox

Lear's decision to divide his kingdom is driven by a desire to maintain power without the burdens of responsibility. In doing so, he sets in motion a power struggle that destroys him and his family. Shakespeare presents a world where power is a double-edged sword: those who seek it often lose themselves in the process, while those who have it are blind to its dangers.

### The Enduring Relevance

_King Lear_'s dualities — idealism and pragmatism, authority and vulnerability, sanity and madness — are as relevant today as they were in Shakespeare's time. In an era where power is often concentrated in the hands of the few, and where truth is increasingly difficult to discern amidst competing voices, _King Lear_ remains a cautionary tale about the dangers of pride, the fragility of human relationships, and the consequences of failing to see the world as it truly is.

Shakespeare holds up a mirror to the human soul, revealing its grandeur and its folly. It is a tragedy not just of one man's downfall but of the universal tensions that shape our lives.
