## Krishnamurti on Meditation and Control

As we navigate through life, it's evident that our world is brimming with religious doctrines and rituals. These tenets, complex and oftentimes rigid, tend to draw a skeptical eyebrow from the intellectual community. This skepticism is not without reason, yet ironically, we find that many, as they approach the twilight of their lives, return to these religious foldings. This tendency stems from a deeply rooted human aspiration for transcendental meaning, a longing to believe in an existence beyond our ordinary, tangible reality.

The vast multitude of philosophies and religions that humanity has spun into existence are testament to this very pursuit. They serve as intellectual intoxicants, imbuing us with the sense of a life beyond the mundane, filling us with hope and meaning. But it begs the question: when venturing into the realm of spiritual and philosophical explorations, why do we instinctively submit to these established doctrines and traditions? Why do we perceive that religious authorities possess something we lack? The answer is simple: there is no such disparity. Every individual is furnished with the necessary ingredients for spiritual exploration - there is no exclusive 'authority' in this sphere.

In this context, consider the practice of meditation, which, in essence, is a process of deep, concentrated contemplation. The same principle applies to the mantra, which encourages us to reflect and shun self-centered activities. No one can 'teach' us these practices - they are innately human, fostered and developed through our personal journeys. Nevertheless, it's vital to acknowledge that these systems, including meditation, are products of human thought.

Being products of thought, these systems are fundamentally limited. No amount of understanding or practicing can truly transcend these limitations. Moreover, the concept of meditation as a predefined routine or a step-by-step process towards a fixed goal can be misleading. There's no predetermined objective awaiting us at the end of a meditation practice. This perceived path is illusory, a mere construct of our minds.

In committing to a regular meditation practice, we risk confining our minds, dulling their vibrant energy into a mechanical monotony, thereby blunting the fine edge of direct perception. Our susceptibility to these practices is fueled by our quest for existential meaning. Nevertheless, the key ingredients needed for genuine spiritual exploration are nothing more than common sense and critical thinking. The first step is to liberate our minds from illusions.

Indeed, meditation, in its purest form, isn't about limiting or conditioning the mind. Rather, it's about engaging the mind in its totality, allowing it to function as a complete, harmonious entity.

The Intersection of Meditation and Contemplation

While contemplation can occur within the confines of our conditioning (a Christian priest, for instance, contemplating within his religious framework), authentic meditation encompasses:

- Unfettered attention
- The absence of specialization
- Disregard for beliefs and ideology, or, in essence, thought.

### The Role of Meditation in a Specialized World

Let's consider the example of a physicist. This individual is deeply rooted in his specialized realm of knowledge. His identity as a physicist, as well as a man, a professor, a father, a husband, and a person with health challenges, inevitably colors his perspective. Therefore, his approach to meditation is likely to be shaped by these identities.

However, it is possible for the physicist to exist in tandem with the meditating human being. Despite our specific roles and identities, we all begin as human beings equipped with a basic consciousness - a primal wholeness that predates any specialization. It is this pre-specialized consciousness that we can tap into through attention, ultimately bridging the divide between our specialized and meditating selves.
