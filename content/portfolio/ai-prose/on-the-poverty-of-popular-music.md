# On the Poverty of Popular Music

Four chords. That is all it takes. Four chords, a verse-chorus-verse structure, a resolution to the fifth, and you have yourself a hit. The formula has been known for decades, and yet it persists, for it sells, and selling is the only metric the industry recognises.

Popular music is standardised in the strictest sense of the word. The harmonic vocabulary is restricted, the melodic range narrow, the themes confined to a handful of categories: romance, heartbreak, sex, and whatever the cultural moment demands as its token of relevance. Even the ornamental deviations (the trills, the vocal runs, the obligatory key change before the final chorus) are themselves standardised. The deviation is anticipated. Surprise has been institutionalised.

But this standardisation operates at a deeper level than mere formula. In serious music (and by serious I mean music composed with the aspiration that every detail derives its sense from the totality of the piece) instruments engage in a dialogue. The dialogue is abstract, dynamic, unrepeatable; even when a passage recurs, its meaning shifts because the context surrounding it has shifted. The detail contains the whole and is an exposition of the whole, produced out of a conception of the whole. A phrase in a Mahler symphony is not interchangeable with a phrase from another. It cannot be. Its sense lies in its position within the architecture of that particular work.

Popular music inverts this principle entirely. The parts do not serve the whole; the whole serves the parts. A catchy bassline or a memorable hook: these are isolated, extractable, and substitutable. You do not listen to a pop song for its architecture. You listen for the moment, the drop, the chorus that lodges in your skull. The rest is connective tissue, disposable and forgotten. This functions as emphasis on certain elements at the expense of the totality, and the totality consequently ceases to exist as a meaningful category.

And who composes these songs? The question is not rhetorical. The publishing apparatus, the label cartels, the streaming algorithms: these are the true authors. The artist is a vessel. The creative impulse, whatever remains of it, is filtered through institutional prescriptions: it must be understood immediately and it must conform to the language of music as the listener already knows it. The unique is translated to the norm, automated for the listener, eliciting conditioned responses. Noncompliance implies exclusion.

Consider what Spotify has done to listening itself. Music is now sorted by mood, by activity, by ambient function. There are playlists for studying and exercising and sleeping. Music has lost its autonomy. It has become a means to an end, setting the ambient atmosphere, accompanying the workout, since it is listened to with general inattention. It assumes a socio-psychological function rather than an aesthetic one. The experience is passive, and passivity is precisely the point. After a hard day of labour, the listener does not want concentration. The listener wants distraction. The role of popular music converges with that of Netflix, of Marvel, of the entire apparatus of passive entertainment: relaxation without effort, stimulation without engagement.

But is this what is demanded? Or is this what the apparatus moulds its consumers to demand? The distinction matters. The capacity for attention and novelty decreases with every scroll through TikTok, every Instagram feed, every curated playlist that spares you the inconvenience of choosing. The scarce leisure time hardly affords the investment that a genuine aesthetic experience requires: the patience of critical listening, of engaging with a musical **Language** on its own terms, of sitting with a piece that does not yield its meaning on first hearing. Music as a Language requires one to speak that Language to appreciate it truly.

And so we arrive at a paradox. The genres (pop, rock, hip-hop, EDM) label the type and the artist, differentiating among the undifferentiated. Any artist can replace Taylor Swift or Justin Bieber, and this is so regardless of talent; the constraints of the form have been tightened to the point where individuality is a liability. The frame of mind to which this music appeals is one of inattention, and it reinforces that inattention with every listen. The musical culture is shaped by the common musical language, and that language is kept frozen. The chance to develop it is not given. The label cartels do not allow it to evolve.

I affirm that a concentrated and conscious experience of art – of any art – requires effort and energy. It is the opposite of the pre-digested commodity designed for those wishing to relieve boredom. The listener must be willing to struggle, to hear something that does not immediately resolve into the familiar. This is what separates aesthetic experience from entertainment, and it is a separation the industry has every incentive to prevent.

What remains, then, is a question each listener must confront for themselves. Are you hearing music, or are you merely being exposed to sound arranged in a pattern you have been trained to tolerate? The difference is the difference between speaking a language and hearing noise; between a life examined and one merely undergone.
