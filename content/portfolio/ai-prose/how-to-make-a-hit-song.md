# Of course. Here is the note converted into a Casually Explained-style transcript.
How to Make a Hit Song
(Scene opens with a stick figure standing next to a large flowchart. The stick figure, MAN, points to the first box.)
MAN: Alright, so you want to make music. This used to be complicated, involving things like ‘talent’ and ‘practice’. Luckily, we’ve optimised the process. Now it's a simple, step-by-step formula for maximum audience digestibility.
(MAN points to the first box on the flowchart, labelled: 'PICK A TOPIC'.)
MAN: Step one: pick your theme. You have three main choices.
(The box expands to show three options with checkboxes: [ ] Romance, [ ] Being at a party, [ ] Vague social awareness.)
MAN: You can write about loving someone, or maybe not loving them anymore. You can write about being at a social gathering, preferably one with dancing. Or, for the advanced user, you can write about how things are… well, how they are. Stick to these. You don’t want to write a song about, say, the migratory patterns of geese. It’s a niche market.
Advanced Song-Making Techniques
(MAN is now standing in front of a diagram of a song, represented by a long rectangle. He points to little decorative squiggles on it.)
MAN: Now, you might think all songs that follow the formula sound the same. And they do. But we have special techniques to make them feel different. Think of these as downloadable content for your song.
(He points to a box labelled 'THE BASSLINE'.)
MAN: A bassline is like the song’s eyebrows. You don’t always notice it, but if it’s weird, the whole face is ruined. Just make it do something simple and catchy. Done.
(He points to another box labelled 'THE HOOK'.)
MAN: The hook is the part of the song designed to get stuck in someone’s head while they're trying to do their taxes. This is the most important part. Make it repetitive. Make it simple enough for a child to remember. Ideally, it should also be the title of the song. Efficiency is key.
(He points to a final box: 'THRILLS AND FILLS'.)
MAN: Even the little drum solos and vocal gymnastics that seem spontaneous are, in fact, following their own tiny rulebooks. A drum fill is just a socially acceptable way for the drummer to hit things a bit randomly before the loud part starts again. It's scheduled chaos.
What About Old Music?
(The scene changes. Two stick-figure instruments, a violin and a cello, are on a stage. They have speech bubbles.)
MAN (V.O.): Then you have what some people call ‘serious’ or ‘classical’ music. This is a different beast. Here, the instruments are all having a conversation. An abstract one. In a language you probably don’t speak.
VIOLIN: (in speech bubble) My dear Cello, do you not feel that this E-flat passage is a poignant commentary on the fleeting nature of existence?
CELLO: (in speech bubble) I don’t know, man. I’m just trying to hold down the low end. Can we just get to the crescendo?
MAN (V.O.): In pop music, every part is designed to be enjoyed on its own. It’s like a snack platter. You can just grab the hook, or the beat. Classical music is more like a stew. If you just eat one carrot out of it, you’ve missed the point. And now you just have a weirdly savoury carrot. The whole thing is designed as one unit. It’s very demanding. It requires you to pay attention.
Art vs. Business
(MAN is now standing between a stick figure ARTIST with a paintbrush and a stick figure PRODUCER in a suit holding a bag of money.)
MAN: Now, the artist will often want to create a rich, flavourful stew. They might want to add… paprika.
ARTIST: I have a vision! A groundbreaking fusion of—
PRODUCER: That’s great. But will it play nicely in a dentist's waiting room? The goal here is to create something that appeals to the broadest possible audience, which means it cannot be challenging. Or surprising. Or have too much paprika.
(The Producer gestures to a huge bell curve. The very top peak is labelled 'LIKES SONGS THAT SOUND LIKE OTHER SONGS'.)
PRODUCER: We’re aiming for the peak. The sweet spot of institutionalised norms. It’s not about limiting artistic freedom; it’s about maximising commercial viability. Which is a much more professional way of saying we want to make money.
The Algorithm is Your God Now
(MAN is shown kneeling before a giant, glowing Spotify logo.)
MAN: This whole system is not new. It’s the same logic that drives television and social media. A constant, steady stream of content that is easy to understand and requires minimal brainpower.
MAN: And then came the streaming services, which perfected the formula. Their algorithms are designed to keep you listening, and the best way to do that is to give you something you already know you like.
(A diagram shows a user listening to a song. An arrow points from the user to the ALGORITHM.)
USER: I like this song.
ALGORITHM: Noted. Here are seven other songs that are 98% identical. Do not attempt to leave. I have also prepared a playlist called 'Chill Afternoon Vibes' which is just this same song played on a ukulele.
MAN (V.O.): In this environment, being different is a risk. If your song is too weird, the algorithm will notice. And it will be banished to the shadow realm of undiscoverable tracks.
So What Does This Do to Us?
(MAN is in his living room. Music notes are floating out of a speaker and bouncing off his head without going in.)
MAN: The result is that music is often treated less like an art form and more like mood lighting. It’s the pleasant background noise for your commute, your workout, your attempt to cook pasta without burning the house down. It’s just… there.
(A stick figure is shown scrolling through TikTok on a phone. A tiny progress bar at the top of the screen labelled 'ATTENTION SPAN' is almost completely empty.)
MAN: This is mostly because we have the attention span of a goldfish that’s just had too much coffee. We’re conditioned to consume content in tiny, 15-second bites. The effort required for a deep aesthetic experience, like listening to a full album, feels monumental. It’s a commitment. In an economy of scarce leisure time, most people will choose passive entertainment. It’s just easier.
Conclusion: How to Listen to Music
(MAN is standing at a crossroads. One path is labelled 'THE HITS PLAYLIST'. The other, much smaller and unpaved path is labelled 'EXPLORE'.)
MAN: So, what’s the takeaway? The modern musical landscape is a complex machine designed to produce standardised, easily consumed products. To really appreciate music, you need to be an active listener. You need to take the time to explore beyond the pre-selected menu, delve into diverse genres, and engage with music that challenges you.
(He pauses, looking down the difficult path, then back at the easy one.)
MAN: Or, you know. You could just put on that ‘Songs to Sing in the Shower’ playlist for the 400th time. It’s got that one song you like. And being comfortable is nice too.
(Final screen with a single line of text.)
TEXT: Pro tip: Just listen to video game soundtracks. They’re designed to be in the background for hours without getting annoying. It’s the final form of music.
