## Anarchy State Utopia - the Origin of State

**(Video opens with 3Blue1Brown's characteristic dark background and evolving geometric shapes. Soft, inquisitive piano music plays.)**

**Narrator (Grant Sanderson's voice, calm and engaging):** Hello, and welcome. Today, we're going to embark on a rather unusual journey. We've previously established the legitimacy of a _minimal_ state – a government limited strictly to protecting individual rights, a concept many find…well, a bit underwhelming. It lacks the _robustness_, the perceived power and scope, of the states we see around us.

**(The geometric shapes morph into a simple stick figure representation of a person, then another, then a small group, representing the "state of nature." A simple box appears around them, labelled "Minimal State.")**

The question we'll tackle is this: Can we, through a series of logically consistent and morally permissible steps – steps that _don't_ violate anyone's rights – move from this bare-bones minimal state to something resembling the complex, powerful states we inhabit today? If we _can_, it might illuminate the very nature of those states, revealing their underlying structure.

**(The box representing the "Minimal State" expands slightly, with a question mark inside.)**

But before we dive in, we need to address a fundamental problem in moral and philosophical arguments: the difficulty of using parallel examples to convince someone.

**(A new scene appears: two simple drawings. One shows a cat, the other a slightly different cat. Labels appear: "Case A" and "Case B.")**

Imagine I want to change your mind about Case A. I present Case B, a parallel example. If Case B is _too_ different, you'll simply dismiss it. If it's _too_ close, you'll likely view it through the lens of your pre-existing opinion on Case A. You'll say, "Well, that's not so bad, because it's just like…" and then you'll justify your original stance.

**(A series of progressively closer cat drawings appears, illustrating the spectrum of similarity.)**

This is similar to the problem with deductive arguments, where someone might reject a premise rather than accept an unwelcome conclusion. But with deductive arguments, a long chain of reasoning can help, starting with premises everyone agrees on. Parallel examples, to be convincing, need to be strikingly similar, which makes them vulnerable to this "contamination of judgment."

**(The screen shows a branching tree diagram, representing a deductive argument, then shrinks it to emphasize the single, close parallel example again.)**

You might try a chain of _increasingly_ similar examples, hoping to isolate the judgment. But people rarely find "draw the line" arguments persuasive. They'll always claim the line falls _just_ beyond their firmly held belief.

**(A spectrum of cats appears again, with a hand attempting to draw a vertical line through it, but the line keeps shifting.)**

The strongest case would be a _perfectly_ parallel example that is undeniably clear _on its own merits_. But finding such "lovely examples" is incredibly difficult. And even then, you'd need to explain any subtle differences and why those differences _don't_ matter for the argument.

**(A single, beautifully drawn cat appears, glowing slightly, then is juxtaposed with a slightly less perfect cat.)**

This ties into a larger puzzle about consistency arguments. Philosophers of science often claim that for any given data set, there are _infinite_ possible explanations.

**(The screen displays a graph with a few data points. Multiple curves are drawn through the points, illustrating the infinite possibilities.)**

Think about it: you can draw an infinite number of curves through a finite set of points. But has anyone actually _proven_ there's even _one_ explanation for every data set, let alone an infinite number? We lack a precise definition of the "explanatory relation," making this claim difficult to assess.

**(The graph fades, replaced by the words "Explanatory Relation: E" and a question mark.)**

Similarly, in ethics, we have moral judgments and moral principles (analogous to laws in science). Is it plausible that any set of moral judgments can be explained by an _infinite_ number of moral principles?

**(The words "Moral Judgments" and "Moral Principles" appear, connected by a similar "E" relation.)**

The hope that generalization arguments can pinpoint a _single_ correct moral principle seems as optimistic as believing only _one_ scientific law explains a given dataset. Challenging someone to distinguish between two moral judgments they make is like assuming there's _no_ fundamental law that could explain a consistent set of data. These are very strong assumptions.

**(The screen shows a split image: one side with moral judgments, the other with scientific data, both with question marks above them.)**

A more modest claim might be that _within a person's existing moral concepts_, there must be some fundamental principle accounting for their judgments. They can't just say, "Oh, a moral genius could invent new concepts." We'd need to delve into _why_ that isn't a satisfactory response, but it seems like a more manageable challenge.

**(The screen focuses on the words "Existing Moral Concepts," emphasizing a limited framework.)**

Now, keeping these difficulties with parallel examples in mind – and I urge you to be aware of your own potential biases – let's return to our main task: deriving a more-than-minimal state.

**(The original stick figures and the "Minimal State" box reappear.)**

We start in the state of nature. Property is acquired justly, and then transferred through exchange or gifts. Property rights themselves are shaped by the need to internalize externalities – the positive and negative effects of our actions on others.

**(The stick figures begin exchanging simple objects. Arrows appear, representing positive and negative externalities.)**

Internalizing _negative_ externalities is relatively straightforward: you compensate others for the harm your actions cause to their property. But what about _positive_ externalities? Full internalization, in its strongest form, would mean you receive the _full_ benefit of your actions to others – essentially, others paying you back for any benefit they receive.

**(A stick figure performs an action. Arrows representing benefit flow to other figures, then arrows representing "payback" flow back to the original figure.)**

At first, this seems to eliminate all benefits of living in society! But, in a free market, competition to provide benefits would emerge. The resulting price would be lower than the maximum a recipient would pay, creating a "consumer surplus" – a genuine benefit of social interaction.

**(A graph appears, showing supply and demand curves, highlighting the "consumer surplus" area.)**

Even in a non-free market, with some other selection mechanism, there would still be benefit: you'd receive full payback for the benefits _you_ provide. However, the system becomes incoherent if we push it further. You benefit from living in a society where others repay you. Should _that_ benefit be internalized too? Do you pay back your expected payback? This creates an infinite regress.

**(A complex, looping diagram appears, illustrating the infinite regress of payback for payback.)**

Economists don't usually focus on this strong principle of full payback. They're concerned with _sufficient_ payback to cover the costs of the activity, ensuring it happens. This _weak_ form is what's relevant for economic efficiency.

**(The graph reappears, highlighting the area where benefits exceed costs.)**

Now, let's get to the core of our derivation. People don't view ownership as simply _having_ a thing, but as possessing a bundle of _rights_ concerning that thing. These rights are separable.

**(A simple object appears, and branching lines show different rights associated with it: "Right to Use," "Right to Sell," "Right to Modify," etc.)**

Multiple people can hold different rights over the same object. Several people can _jointly_ own a right, using a decision procedure to exercise it. Now imagine the economic situation is that free market and charity greatly reduce destitution, but it still exists. Some people desire more.

**(The stick figures reappear, some with more possessions than others.)**

And here's where things get…interesting. Some people hit upon the idea of _incorporating themselves_ – selling shares in their own lives!

**(A stick figure is surrounded by a grid, representing shares of stock.)**

They partition the rights they previously held over themselves into a vast array of discrete rights: the right to choose their occupation, their clothing, their spouse, their location, their recreational drug use, their reading material…and so on.

**(A long list of rights appears, scrolling across the screen.)**

Some rights they retain, others they sell. Initially, it's a joke, a novelty. But then, people see more serious possibilities. They sell rights that could genuinely benefit others: occupational licensure rights, import-control rights, drug rights, tax rights, vice rights, draft rights, wage-price-control rights, anti-discrimination rights, subpoena rights, even physical equality rights (the right to requisition body parts for transplants!).

**(The list of rights continues, becoming increasingly extensive and potentially oppressive.)**

For various reasons, people _buy_ these shares. This creates a market for control over individuals. Perhaps no one is completely enslaved, but many become heavily dominated by a single shareholder or small group. This domination, arising from a series of _voluntary_ exchanges, is, strictly speaking, not _unjust_. But it's certainly _undesirable_.

**(Stick figures are shown being controlled by others, with strings representing ownership.)**

To combat this, newly incorporating individuals add restrictions to their stock, preventing anyone from owning too many shares. Over time, ownership becomes more dispersed. Almost everyone sells off most rights, retaining just _one_ share of each, "to have some say," as they put it.

**(The controlling strings become thinner and more numerous, spreading out to many stick figures.)**

The sheer number of shares and their dispersal leads to chaos. Constant stockholder meetings are held to make decisions about everything. New occupations emerge: "stockholders' representative." Consolidation movements arise, combining meetings based on individuals or specific rights. But it's still incredibly unwieldy.

**(A chaotic scene appears, with countless stick figures attending various meetings.)**

The price of shares plummets. People start collecting them like baseball cards. Finally, a great consolidation convention is held. After days of frantic trading, a remarkable outcome emerges: _each person owns exactly one share in each right over every other person, including themselves_.

**(A grand, unified meeting appears, with each stick figure holding a single share.)**

Now, there can be _one_ meeting to decide everything for everyone. Initially, everyone attends, but it's too large and inefficient. Eventually, only those with a certain number of votes (through proxies) can attend.

**(The meeting becomes smaller, with some stick figures representing others.)**

The issue of children arises. A "Great Corporation Share" is a valuable asset. To avoid leaving children powerless, a system of "splitting" is introduced. Shares revert to the Board of Directors upon death, and existing shares split to create new shares for those coming of age. These new shares are exchanged for the youngsters incorporating themselves and signing over _all_ their self-ownership to the corporation.

**(A diagram appears, illustrating the share-splitting process.)**

It's presented as a perfectly even trade: you give up one full share in yourself and gain a tiny fraction of ownership in _everyone_ else, plus yourself. People genuinely believe that when everyone owns everyone, nobody owns anybody. It's the "rule of impersonal regulations," not the "rule of men." Fraternity flourishes.

**(Stick figures are shown embracing, interconnected by lines representing shared ownership.)**

But…a few malcontents refuse to join. They call the system unjust. The Board of Directors considers forceful action, but realizes they haven't been explicitly granted that power. Some argue for tacit consent, but that's quickly dismissed. Others try a bizarre argument about parental ownership extending to the corporation, but it's too outlandish.

**(A small group of stick figures stands apart, protesting.)**

We pause our narrative to consider Locke's views on parental ownership. Locke had to refute Filmer's claim that parents own their children, which seemingly followed from Locke's own property theory. Locke's arguments are complex and, frankly, not entirely convincing. He struggles to explain why parents _don't_ own their children, given his principles.

**(The screen displays excerpts from Locke's writings, highlighting the relevant arguments.)**

Returning to our tale, it's decided that the youngsters _don't_ have to join. They can leave the corporation's area (though that's practically impossible). The dissenters claim they can buy land _within_ the corporation's territory and live independently. But the corporation's rules, adopted at the great convention, prohibit secession.

**(The protesting stick figures point to a patch of land within the larger corporate area.)**

Can the corporation allow another corporation to spring up within it? Can it tolerate "ancorpy" – individuals not subject to its control? Some suggest allowing the recalcitrant individuals to opt out of the system _while remaining within the territory_, choosing their own interactions, paying for what they receive.

**(A small, independent enclave is shown within the larger corporate area.)**

But others argue this would be too chaotic, undermining the system. It might tempt others to leave. Who would be left? Only the most vulnerable. How would fraternity flourish? Almost everyone views their system – demoktesis, ownership _of_ the people, _by_ the people, and _for_ the people – as the highest form of social life.

**(The unified meeting reappears, with an emphasis on the collective.)**

And there we have it. Through this bizarre, yet logically consistent and (arguably) morally permissible sequence of steps, we've arrived at something resembling a modern, democratic state, with its vast powers over its citizens. Our hypothetical account has, hopefully, given us a new perspective on the _essential nature_ of such a state.

**(The screen displays the words "Demoktesis: Ownership of the People, By the People, and For the People.")**

To further illustrate the point, let's consider a final, chilling thought experiment: the Tale of the Slave.

**(A series of numbered panels appears, each depicting a stage in the Tale of the Slave, as described in the original notes.)**

1. A slave is completely at the mercy of a brutal master.
2. The master is kinder, beating the slave only for specific infractions.
3. The master allocates resources among his slaves based on need and merit.
4. The master allows slaves four days of freedom, requiring three days of work.
5. The master allows slaves to work elsewhere, requiring three-sevenths of their wages.
6. The master allows all slaves _except you_ to vote on how to use the collected wages and restrict your activities.
7. You are allowed to participate in the discussions, but not vote.
8. You are allowed to vote _only_ if the others are deadlocked.
9. Your vote is counted equally with the others, but only matters in a tie.

**(The panels progress, showing the gradual increase in the slave's "freedom.")**

The crucial question is: at which point does this _cease_ to be the tale of a slave?

**(The final panel shows the slave casting a ballot, with a large question mark superimposed.)**

Finally, let's briefly consider how these hypothetical histories should affect our judgment of existing societies.

**(A flow chart appears, representing different scenarios.)**

If a society arose from a just actual history, it's just. If the actual history is unjust, and _no_ just hypothetical history could lead to its current structure, it's unjust. Things get complicated when the actual history is unjust, but a _just_ hypothetical history _could_ have led to the same structure.

If the hypothetical history is "close" to the actual one, and the injustices didn't significantly shape the structure, the structure is probably as just as we can expect. If the hypothetical history involves consent that _didn't_ actually happen, or wouldn't happen now, the structure is unjust.

If the just process _doesn't_ involve consent, we evaluate the structure based on the process itself. If that process is better (in ways other than justice) than the actual history, it improves our view of the structure. But if the just process requires despicable individuals, it _doesn't_ improve our view.

Structures closer to the individual rights defined by moral side constraints are more just than those further away. If a structure embodying only individual rights can arise justly, we should be willing to stick with it, even if it _did_ arise unjustly (after rectifying specific injustices). But if a structure deviates from those rights, we shouldn't let it continue, even if a just hypothetical history _could_ have created it. The situation of individual rights must be re-established.
