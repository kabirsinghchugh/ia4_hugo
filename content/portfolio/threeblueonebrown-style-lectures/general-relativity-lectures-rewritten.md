## Einstein General Relativity

We'll start with the first lecture, focusing on the foundational concepts.

**Lecture 1: The Geometry of Spacetime - A New Way to See Motion**

**(logo, transitioning to a simple animation of a curved sheet of paper)**

We're about to dive into the fascinating world of General Relativity. But forget everything you _think_ you know about gravity, about space, about time. We're going to rebuild it all, from the ground up, with a fresh perspective.

**(Animation: A single point appears on the curved sheet)**

Imagine this curved surface. It represents something profound: **spacetime**. Not just space, not just time, but a unified entity, a four-dimensional fabric woven together. For simplicity, we're only showing two dimensions here, but keep in mind the real thing has four: three spatial dimensions and one time dimension.

**(Animation: A curve is drawn on the surface, representing a worldline)**

Now, see this curve? This isn't just any line; it represents the entire history of an object – let's say, an apple – through spacetime. We call this a **worldline**. It's the path the apple takes not just through space, but also through time.

**(Animation: The worldline is divided into equal segments, marked with points)**

To understand this worldline, let's slice it up. We divide it into equally spaced intervals, like marking milestones on a road. We pick a starting point – our origin – and then number these intervals: 1, 2, 3, and so on.

**(Animation: The points on the worldline are highlighted, and the concept of "proper time" is introduced with the symbol τ)**

What we've done is create a _graduation_ along the curve. This graduation represents something crucial: **proper time**, denoted by the Greek letter τ (tau). Think of proper time as the time measured by a clock _carried by the apple itself_. It's the apple's own personal timeline, ticking away as it moves through spacetime. Each tick marks an equal interval of the apple's own experienced time.

**(Animation: The focus shifts to emphasize the idea of _motion_ through spacetime)**

Notice something profound: The worldline, initially just a static curve, now represents _motion_. It's not just where the apple _is_; it's the apple's _journey_ through spacetime. We've inherently linked space and time together to define motion in a new way.

**(Animation: A coordinate grid is overlaid on the curved surface)**

Now, we want to _describe_ this motion mathematically. To do that, we need a way to label points. We introduce a **coordinate system** – a grid laid down on our spacetime surface. This grid has an origin (a zero point) and axes.

**(Animation: The grid lines are numbered, and the concept of coordinates is explained)**

Each point on the grid, and therefore each point on the apple's worldline, can now be described by a set of numbers: its **coordinates**. In our 2D example, we need two numbers. In 4D spacetime, we'd need four.

**(Key Point - Animation emphasizes the arbitrariness of the coordinate system)**

**Crucially**, this grid is _arbitrary_. It's a human construct, a tool we use for description. It has _no inherent physical meaning_. We could choose a different grid, a warped grid, a rotated grid, and the physics of the apple's motion wouldn't change. The _coordinates_ would change, but the underlying reality – the apple's worldline – would remain the same.

**(Animation: Transition to a specific example – an apple falling towards Earth, viewed from a distance)**

Let's make this concrete. Imagine an apple falling straight down towards Earth. We, as distant observers, are watching this happen. We can measure two things:

1. **Distance (r):** The distance between the apple and the center of the Earth.
2. **Time (t):** The time elapsed on _our_ clock.

**(Animation: A coordinate system (t, r) is shown, with the apple's worldline drawn as a curve within it)**

These two measurements, _r_ and _t_, become our coordinates. They locate the apple in spacetime _relative to our perspective_. Notice the distinction: _t_ is _our_ time, a coordinate, while τ (tau) is the apple's _proper_ time, ticking along its worldline.

**(Animation: A point on the apple's worldline is highlighted, showing its (t, r) coordinates)**

For instance, the apple might pass through a point where _t_ = 1 second and _r_ = 7000 kilometers. This means that when _our_ clock reads 1 second, we measure the apple to be 7000 kilometers from Earth's center.

**(Animation: An apple at rest on Earth's surface, highlighting its motion through time)**

Finally, a critical insight: _Nothing is ever truly stationary in spacetime_. Even an apple resting on the Earth's surface is moving – it's moving _through time_. Every object has a "speed" in spacetime; it's just that this speed can be distributed differently between the spatial dimensions and the time dimension.

**(End of Lecture 1 - Summary and Preview)**

We've laid the groundwork. We've introduced spacetime, worldlines, proper time, and coordinate systems. We've seen how motion is fundamentally linked to the geometry of spacetime. In the next lecture, we'll delve into the concept of _velocity_ in spacetime and start building the equations that govern motion in this new framework. Get ready to see the world in a whole new light!

**(Intro Music with animated 3Blue1Brown style logo, transitioning to a recap of Lecture 1)**

Welcome back! Last time, we introduced the revolutionary idea of spacetime, worldlines representing an object's journey through it, and the crucial concept of proper time – the time measured by the object itself. We also learned how to use arbitrary coordinate systems to describe locations in spacetime. Now, we're ready to take the next step: understanding **velocity** in this four-dimensional world.

**(Animation: Recap of a worldline on a curved surface, with coordinate grid)**

Recall our apple, tracing its worldline through spacetime. We described its position using coordinates, like _t_ (our time) and _r_ (distance from Earth). But these coordinates, while useful, are just labels. They don't directly tell us how the apple is _moving_ through spacetime. For that, we need velocity.

**(Animation: A tangent vector is drawn at a point on the worldline, representing spacetime velocity)**

At any point along the worldline, we can draw an arrow – a **vector** – that's _tangent_ to the curve. This vector represents the apple's **spacetime velocity**. It points in the direction the apple is moving in spacetime, and its length represents _how fast_ it's moving.

**(Animation: The vector's length is emphasized, and the concept of the speed of light 'c' is introduced)**

Here's a mind-bending fact: The _length_ of this velocity vector is _always_ the same for _all_ objects. It's a universal constant, which we call the **speed of light**, denoted by _c_.

**(Equation 1: ||v|| = c is displayed and explained)**

Let's write our first equation:

||**v**|| = _c_

This equation states that the magnitude (or norm) of the velocity vector, **v**, is _always_ equal to the speed of light. This is a cornerstone of relativity.

**(Animation: Explanation of units where c = 1)**

To simplify things, physicists often use units where _c_ = 1. For example, we could measure time in seconds and distance in _light-seconds_ (the distance light travels in one second). In these units, the equation becomes even simpler: ||**v**|| = 1. This beautifully highlights the interconnectedness of space and time: time _is_ a measure of distance in spacetime.

**(Animation: Decomposition of the velocity vector into components along the coordinate axes)**

Now, let's connect this spacetime velocity to our coordinate system. Remember our grid? We can draw two arrows, called **basis vectors**, aligned with the coordinate axes at the point where our velocity vector is. Let's call these basis vectors **e**₀ (along the time axis) and **e**₁ (along the spatial axis).

**(Animation: The velocity vector is shown as the sum of multiples of the basis vectors)**

We can express the velocity vector, **v**, as a _sum_ of these basis vectors, each multiplied by a certain number. These numbers are called the **components** of the velocity vector. For example:

**v** = 2**e**₀ + 1**e**₁

This means the apple is moving "two units" along the time direction and "one unit" along the spatial direction, _per unit of its own proper time_.

**(General Equation for Velocity Components: v = v⁰e₀ + v¹e₁ is displayed)**

More generally, we can write:

**v** = _v_⁰**e**₀ + _v_¹**e**₁

Where _v_⁰ and _v_¹ are the components of the velocity vector along the time and space axes, respectively. These components tell us the _rate of change_ of the coordinates with respect to proper time.

- _v_⁰ = d_t_/dτ (How fast _our_ time changes per unit of the apple's proper time)
- _v_¹ = d_r_/dτ (How fast the apple's distance changes per unit of its proper time)

**(Introduction of Einstein Summation Notation)**

Now, a bit of mathematical elegance. In relativity, we often deal with sums over all coordinates. To avoid writing out long sums repeatedly, we use **Einstein summation notation**.

**(Explanation of Einstein Summation Notation: v = v<sup>α</sup>e<sub>α</sub>)**

Instead of writing:

**v** = _v_⁰**e**₀ + _v_¹**e**₁

We write:

**v** = _v_<sup>α</sup>**e**<sub>α</sub>

Here's the rule: When a Greek index (like α) appears _twice_ in a term, once as a superscript and once as a subscript, it implies summation over all possible values of that index. In our 2D example, α would take on the values 0 and 1, automatically generating the sum we wrote earlier.

**(Animation: Transition to the example of a satellite in circular orbit)**

Let's apply this to a satellite orbiting Earth. We can describe its motion using two coordinates:

1. **Time (t):** Our time, measured on our clock.
2. **Angle (φ):** The angle the satellite makes with a reference line, describing its position around the Earth.

**(Animation: Worldline of the satellite, velocity vector, and its decomposition into components)**

The satellite's worldline is a helix in spacetime. We can draw its velocity vector and decompose it into components along the _t_ and φ axes.

- _v_<sup>t</sup> = d_t_/dτ: The _temporal speed_ – how many seconds of _our_ time pass for each second of the satellite's proper time.
- _v_<sup>φ</sup> = dφ/dτ: The _angular speed_ – how many degrees the satellite sweeps out per second of its proper time.

**(Animation: Highlighting the problem – the Pythagorean theorem doesn't work directly with components)**

Now, here's the crucial point. You might be tempted to use the Pythagorean theorem to calculate the length of the velocity vector (which we know is _c_) from its components:

( _v_<sup>t</sup> )² + ( _v_<sup>φ</sup> )² = _c_² ? **NO!**

**(Animation: Two satellites at different altitudes with the same angular speed, but different actual speeds)**

This is _wrong_ in general. Why? Because our coordinates are _arbitrary_. They don't necessarily represent true distances. Imagine two satellites at different altitudes, both with the same _angular speed_ (dφ/dτ). The satellite farther out is clearly moving _faster_ in terms of actual distance covered, even though its _v_<sup>φ</sup> component is the same.

**(End of Lecture 2 - Summary and Preview)**

We've defined spacetime velocity, learned how to decompose it into components using a coordinate system, and discovered a fundamental problem: our coordinates don't directly measure distances. We cannot use the simple Pythagorean theorem. In the next lecture, we'll tackle this problem head-on. We'll introduce the concept of _geodesics_ – the "straightest possible paths" in spacetime – and begin to build a more general version of the Pythagorean theorem that works for _any_ coordinate system. This will lead us to the heart of General Relativity!

**(Intro Music with animated 3Blue1Brown style logo, transitioning to a recap of Lecture 2)**

Welcome back! Last time, we unlocked the concept of spacetime velocity – a vector tangent to an object's worldline, always with a magnitude equal to the speed of light. We also saw that naively using the Pythagorean theorem with coordinate components doesn't work, because coordinates are arbitrary and don't directly represent physical distances. Today, we fix that. We're going to explore the idea of **geodesics** and start building the machinery to measure distances correctly in _any_ coordinate system.

**(Animation: Recap of a worldline on a flat surface, with a velocity vector being transported along itself)**

Imagine a perfectly flat surface. If an object moves without any external forces, it naturally travels in a straight line. We can visualize this by taking the velocity vector and "transporting" it along itself. The vector doesn't change direction or magnitude; it just slides along the straight path.

**(Animation: The concept of a geodesic as the "straightest possible path" in curved spacetime)**

Now, imagine a _curved_ surface, like the surface of a sphere. What's the "straightest possible path" an object can take? It's not a straight line in the usual sense, because the surface itself is curved. Instead, it's a path where the velocity vector, as much as possible, _doesn't change direction relative to the surface_. This path is called a **geodesic**.

**(Animation: A geodesic on a sphere (a great circle) is shown, with the velocity vector being transported along it)**

On a sphere, geodesics are _great circles_ – circles whose centers coincide with the center of the sphere. Think of airplane routes; they often follow great circles to minimize travel distance. As the velocity vector moves along the great circle, it remains "as straight as possible" _with respect to the curved surface_.

**(Animation: The concept of zero acceleration in spacetime – the derivative of the velocity vector is zero along a geodesic)**

Crucially, an object moving along a geodesic experiences _no acceleration_ in spacetime. Its velocity vector, in a sense, remains "constant" _relative to the geometry of spacetime_. We can express this mathematically:

d**v**/dτ = 0

This equation says that the derivative of the velocity vector, **v**, with respect to proper time, τ, is zero along a geodesic. This is a fundamental statement about how objects move freely in spacetime.

**(Animation: The velocity vector is decomposed into components, reminding the viewer of the previous lecture)**

Remember from last time that we can decompose the velocity vector into components using our coordinate system's basis vectors:

**v** = _v_<sup>α</sup>**e**<sub>α</sub>

**(Animation: The derivative of the velocity vector is expanded using the product rule)**

Now, let's take the derivative of this expression with respect to proper time. Remember the product rule from calculus: d(uv)/dx = u(dv/dx) + v(du/dx). Applying this, we get:

d**v**/dτ = (d_v_<sup>α</sup>/dτ)**e**<sub>α</sub> + _v_<sup>α</sup>(d**e**<sub>α</sub>/dτ)

This looks complicated, but it's a crucial step. The first term represents the change in the _components_ of the velocity vector. The second term represents the change in the _basis vectors_ themselves.

**(Animation: The basis vectors are shown changing along a curved coordinate grid)**

This second term is _essential_ on a curved surface (or in a curved coordinate system). The basis vectors, which define the directions of our coordinate axes, can _change_ as we move along the worldline. The grid itself might be warped or twisted.

**(Animation: The change in a basis vector is decomposed along the coordinate directions, introducing Christoffel symbols)**

How do we describe the change in a basis vector? We can express the change in a basis vector, d**e**<sub>α</sub>/dτ, as a sum of changes along _each_ coordinate direction, multiplied by how fast we're moving along those directions:

d**e**<sub>α</sub>/dτ = (∂**e**<sub>α</sub>/∂x<sup>μ</sup>) _v_<sup>μ</sup>

Here:

- ∂**e**<sub>α</sub>/∂x<sup>μ</sup> represents the _rate of change_ of the basis vector **e**<sub>α</sub> as we move along the coordinate direction x<sup>μ</sup>.
- v<sup>μ</sup> is how fast we're moving along x<sup>μ</sup>.
- We are using Einstein notation here! Both Alpha and Mu are being summed over.

**(Animation: The components of the change in a basis vector are defined as Christoffel symbols (Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub>))**

This derivative, ∂**e**<sub>α</sub>/∂x<sup>μ</sup>, is itself a vector. We can decompose it into _its_ components, which we denote with a special symbol: Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub> (Gamma). These are called **Christoffel symbols**.

∂**e**<sub>α</sub>/∂x<sup>μ</sup> = Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub> **e**<sub>λ</sub>

The Christoffel symbols, Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub>, tell us _how much the basis vectors change_ as we move along the coordinate directions. They are a measure of how "curved" our coordinate system is.

**(Animation: Putting it all together – the geodesic equation is derived)**

Substituting everything back into our equation for d**v**/dτ = 0, and doing some algebraic manipulation (using the fact that the basis vectors are linearly independent), we arrive at the **geodesic equation**:

d²x<sup>λ</sup>/dτ² + Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub> (dx<sup>μ</sup>/dτ)(dx<sup>α</sup>/dτ) = 0

This is a _huge_ result! This equation tells us how the _coordinates_ of an object change as it moves along a geodesic – a "straight" path – in spacetime.

- d²x<sup>λ</sup>/dτ² represents the _acceleration_ of the coordinate x<sup>λ</sup>.
- Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub> are the Christoffel symbols, encoding the curvature of our coordinate system.
- (dx<sup>μ</sup>/dτ) and (dx<sup>α</sup>/dτ) are the components of the velocity.

**(Animation: Example: Airplane flying on a great circle (geodesic) on Earth's surface)**

Let's visualize this with our airplane example. The airplane flies along a great circle – a geodesic on the Earth's sphere. Even though the airplane is moving "straight ahead" in spacetime, its _latitude and longitude coordinates_ are constantly changing. The geodesic equation, using the appropriate Christoffel symbols for a sphere, would perfectly describe this curved path in terms of latitude and longitude.

**(Animation: Highlighting that Christoffel symbols measure the "curvedness" of the coordinates, not necessarily the spacetime itself)**

It's important to emphasize: The Christoffel symbols describe the _curvedness of our coordinate system_, not necessarily the curvature of spacetime itself. Even in flat spacetime, we could choose a curved coordinate system, and the Christoffel symbols would be non-zero.

**(End of Lecture 3 - Summary and Preview)**

We've made significant progress! We've defined geodesics, derived the geodesic equation, and introduced the Christoffel symbols. We now have a way to predict the motion of objects in _any_ coordinate system, as long as we know the Christoffel symbols. But how do we _find_ those Christoffel symbols? That's where the **metric tensor** comes in – the star of our next lecture. The metric tensor will finally allow us to measure distances and angles correctly in spacetime, and it will provide the key to calculating the Christoffel symbols. Get ready to unlock the true geometry of spacetime!

**(Intro Music with animated 3Blue1Brown style logo, transitioning to a recap of Lecture 3)**

Welcome back! Last time, we journeyed into the heart of curved spaces and discovered geodesics – the "straightest possible paths." We derived the geodesic equation, which governs motion along these paths, and introduced the enigmatic Christoffel symbols, which encode the "curvedness" of our coordinate system. But we still had a missing piece: how to _calculate_ those Christoffel symbols. Today, we fill that gap. We're going to introduce the **metric tensor**, the key to unlocking the true geometry of spacetime.

**(Animation: Recap of a curved coordinate grid, highlighting the problem of measuring distances)**

Recall our fundamental problem: coordinates are arbitrary. The numerical difference between two coordinate values doesn't necessarily correspond to the actual _distance_ between those points in spacetime. A grid could be stretched, squished, or twisted, and the coordinate differences would be misleading.

**(Animation: Two nearby points on the curved surface, with coordinate differences dx⁰ and dx¹)**

Imagine two points on our curved surface, very close to each other. Let's say their coordinate differences are _dx_⁰ and _dx_¹. We want to find the _actual_ distance, _ds_, between these points.

**(Animation: Attempting to use the Pythagorean theorem and failing – highlighting the need for a more general approach)**

Naively, we might try the Pythagorean theorem:

(_ds_)² = (_dx_⁰)² + (_dx_¹)² ? **NO!**

This only works for a perfectly flat, orthogonal coordinate system. If the grid is distorted, this formula breaks down.

**(Animation: The general form of the squared distance is introduced: (ds)² = g₀₀(dx⁰)² + g₀₁(dx⁰)(dx¹) + g₁₀(dx¹)(dx⁰) + g₁₁(dx¹)² )**

We need a more general expression. It turns out that the square of the distance, (_ds_)², can _always_ be written as a sum of all possible combinations of the coordinate differences, multiplied by some coefficients:

(_ds_)² = _g_₀₀(_dx_⁰)² + _g_₀₁(_dx_⁰)(_dx_¹) + _g_₁₀(_dx_¹)(_dx_⁰) + _g_₁₁(_dx_¹)²

These coefficients, _g_₀₀, _g_₀₁, _g_₁₀, and _g_₁₁, depend on the specific coordinate system and how it's warped or stretched.

**(Animation: The coefficients are arranged into a table – the metric tensor (g<sub>μν</sub>) is introduced)**

We can organize these coefficients into a table, a matrix:

```markdown
[ g₀₀  g₀₁ ]
[ g₁₀  g₁₁ ]
```

This table is called the **metric tensor**, often denoted by _g_<sub>μν</sub>. It's a fundamental object in General Relativity.

**(General Equation for Squared Distance: (ds)² = g<sub>μν</sub>dx<sup>μ</sup>dx<sup>ν</sup> is displayed, using Einstein notation)**

Using Einstein summation notation, we can write the squared distance concisely:

(_ds_)² = _g_<sub>μν</sub> _dx_<sup>μ</sup> _dx_<sup>ν</sup>

Remember, the repeated indices μ and ν imply summation over all coordinate dimensions.

**(Animation: The metric tensor is the key to connecting abstract coordinates to real physical distances and angles)**

**Crucially:** The metric tensor is the bridge between our abstract coordinate system and _real, physical distances and angles_. It tells us how to "weight" the coordinate differences to get the correct distance.

**(Animation: Example: The metric tensor for a flat, Cartesian coordinate system (the identity matrix))**

In a flat, Cartesian coordinate system (like graph paper), the metric tensor is simple:

```markdown
[ 1  0 ]
[ 0  1 ]
```

This gives us back the familiar Pythagorean theorem: (_ds_)² = (_dx_⁰)² + (_dx_¹)²

**(Animation: Example: The metric tensor for a polar coordinate system, showing how it's non-diagonal and depends on the coordinates)**

But in a polar coordinate system, the metric tensor is more complicated:

```markdown
[ 1   0   ]
[ 0   r² ]
```

The length element then becomes,

(_ds_)² = (_dr_)² + r²(_d_φ)²

This reflects the fact that the "distance" corresponding to a change in angle (dφ) depends on how far you are from the origin (r).

**(Animation: The metric tensor allows us to calculate the norm of the velocity vector: c² = g<sub>μν</sub>v<sup>μ</sup>v<sup>ν</sup>)**

Now, we can finally calculate the norm of the velocity vector _correctly_ in _any_ coordinate system. We know the magnitude of the velocity vector is always _c_ (the speed of light). Using the metric tensor, we can write:

_c_² = _g_<sub>μν</sub> _v_<sup>μ</sup> _v_<sup>ν</sup>

This is a generalization of the Pythagorean theorem that works for _any_ spacetime geometry.

**(Animation: Connecting the metric tensor to the Christoffel symbols – a key relationship)**

The metric tensor doesn't just give us distances; it also gives us the _Christoffel symbols_. Remember, the Christoffel symbols describe how the basis vectors change, and the basis vectors are intimately related to the coordinate system. Since the metric tensor encodes the geometry of the coordinate system, it should be no surprise that we can calculate the Christoffel symbols from it.

**(The equation relating Christoffel symbols to derivatives of the metric tensor is displayed (without derivation, but with explanation of terms))**

The precise relationship is a bit messy, but it involves derivatives of the metric tensor components:

Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub> = (1/2) _g_<sup>λρ</sup> (∂_g_<sub>ρα</sub>/∂x<sup>μ</sup> + ∂_g_<sub>ρμ</sub>/∂x<sup>α</sup> - ∂_g_<sub>μα</sub>/∂x<sup>ρ</sup>)

- _g_<sup>λρ</sup> represents the _inverse_ of the metric tensor.
- ∂_g_<sub>…</sub>/∂x<sup>…</sup> represents the partial derivatives of the metric tensor components with respect to the coordinates.

The important thing is: _If we know the metric tensor, we can calculate the Christoffel symbols._

**(Animation: Summary of the complete process: Metric Tensor -> Christoffel Symbols -> Geodesic Equation -> Trajectories)**

Let's recap the chain of logic:

1. **Metric Tensor (g<sub>μν</sub>):** Describes the geometry of spacetime and our coordinate system.
2. **Christoffel Symbols (Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub>):** Calculated from the derivatives of the metric tensor. They tell us how the coordinate grid is curved.
3. **Geodesic Equation:** Uses the Christoffel symbols to predict the motion of objects along geodesics (the "straightest possible paths").
4. **Trajectories:** We can solve the geodesic equation to find the actual paths objects take through spacetime.

**(Animation: Examples: Earth's surface (latitude/longitude), empty spacetime (Minkowski metric))**

Let's see some examples:

- **Earth's Surface (Latitude/Longitude):** We can write down the metric tensor for the surface of a sphere in terms of latitude and longitude. From this, we can calculate the Christoffel symbols and, in principle, find the geodesics (great circles).
- **Minkowski Metric** This is the simplest metric of them all describing a flat space time, where all Christoffel symbols become zero.

We've reached a major milestone! We've introduced the metric tensor, the cornerstone of measuring distances and angles in spacetime. We've seen how it connects to the Christoffel symbols and, ultimately, to the geodesic equation. We now have a powerful framework for describing motion in curved spacetime. But one crucial question remains: _What determines the metric tensor itself?_ What dictates the geometry of spacetime? The answer, of course, is _gravity_ – or, more precisely, the distribution of energy and momentum in the universe. In our final lecture, we'll unveil the **Einstein field equations**, the equations that connect the curvature of spacetime to the matter and energy within it. We'll finally see the full glory of General Relativity!

**(Intro Music with animated 3Blue1Brown style logo, transitioning to a recap of Lecture 4)**

Welcome back! We've come a long way. We've built up a powerful framework for describing motion in curved spacetime, using the metric tensor to measure distances, the Christoffel symbols to characterize coordinate system curvature, and the geodesic equation to predict trajectories. But the fundamental question remains: what _determines_ the metric tensor? What shapes the geometry of spacetime? The answer, beautifully encapsulated in Einstein's theory, is: **the distribution of energy and momentum.** Today, we'll finally unveil the **Einstein field equations**, the heart of General Relativity.

**(Animation: Recap of the metric tensor and its role in determining distances and Christoffel symbols)**

Recall that the metric tensor, _g_<sub>μν</sub>, is our key to unlocking the geometry of spacetime. It allows us to calculate distances and, from its derivatives, the Christoffel symbols. But the metric tensor itself has remained, until now, a given – something we _assumed_ we knew.

**(Animation: A flat sheet representing spacetime is shown. Then, a heavy object is placed on it, causing it to curve.)**

Imagine a flat, rubber sheet. This represents flat spacetime. Now, place a heavy ball on the sheet. The ball creates a dip, a curvature in the sheet. This is the essence of General Relativity: _matter and energy curve spacetime_.

**(Animation: The concept of curvature is visualized – parallel lines converging on a sphere)**

To understand how to describe this curvature mathematically, let's revisit the concept of curvature itself. On a flat surface, parallel lines remain parallel forever. But on a curved surface, like a sphere, parallel lines eventually converge. This convergence is a direct manifestation of curvature.

**(Animation: Transporting a vector around a loop on a flat surface vs. a curved surface. The difference in the final vector on the curved surface defines curvature.)**

A more precise way to define curvature involves _parallel transport_. Imagine taking a vector and transporting it around a closed loop on a surface. On a flat surface, the vector returns to its original orientation. But on a curved surface, the vector _changes direction_ after being transported around the loop. This change in the vector is a direct measure of the curvature.

**(Animation: The Riemann curvature tensor (R<sup>ρ</sup><sub>σμν</sub>) is introduced (without full derivation, but with explanation of its meaning).)**

Mathematically, this curvature is captured by the **Riemann curvature tensor**, denoted by _R_<sup>ρ</sup><sub>σμν</sub>. This is a rather complicated object (with 256 components in 4D spacetime!), but its essence is this: it describes how much a vector changes when parallel-transported around an infinitesimal loop. The Riemann tensor is calculated from the second derivatives of the metric tensor, and the first derivatives combined which is to say, from the Christoffel symbols and their derivatives.

**(Animation: The Ricci tensor (R<sub>μν</sub>) is introduced as a contraction of the Riemann tensor.)**

The Riemann tensor is powerful but unwieldy. We can create a simpler, more manageable object by a process called _contraction_. We essentially "sum" over certain indices of the Riemann tensor to obtain the **Ricci tensor**, _R_<sub>μν</sub>. The Ricci tensor still captures essential information about the curvature, but it has fewer components (16 in 4D spacetime). Intuitively, the Ricci tensor describes how _volumes_ change in curved spacetime.

**(Animation: The Ricci scalar (R) is introduced as a further contraction of the Ricci tensor.)**

## Einstein General Relativity

**Lecture 1: The Geometry of Spacetime**

General Relativity views gravity, space, and time differently than we might expect. Let us explore this view.

Consider a surface, perhaps curved. This surface represents **spacetime** – not merely space, nor only time, but a unified, four-dimensional fabric. We picture two dimensions here for clarity, though spacetime itself has three of space and one of time.

A curve drawn on this surface traces the history of an object, perhaps an apple, through spacetime. This path is its **worldline**. We can mark intervals along this worldline, like milestones on a road. Starting from an origin point, we number them: 1, 2, 3, and so on.

These marks represent **proper time**, denoted τ (tau). This is the time measured by a clock the apple itself carries; each tick marks an equal interval of the apple's own experienced time. The worldline, marked by proper time, now shows the apple's motion – its journey through spacetime.

To describe this journey mathematically, we lay a **coordinate system** – a grid – upon spacetime. This grid has an origin and axes, allowing us to assign a set of numbers, or **coordinates**, to each point on the apple's worldline. In our two-dimensional picture, we need two numbers; in four-dimensional spacetime, we need four.

This grid, however, is merely a tool for description; it is arbitrary. We could choose another grid, perhaps warped or rotated, and the apple's actual path, its worldline, would remain unchanged. Only our numerical description, the coordinates, would differ.

Consider an apple falling towards Earth. We, watching from afar, can measure its distance *r* from Earth's center and the time *t* elapsed on our clock. These measurements, *r* and *t*, serve as coordinates locating the apple in spacetime from our viewpoint. For example, the apple might pass the point where *t* = 1 second and *r* = 7000 kilometers. Note that *t* is our coordinate time, distinct from the apple's proper time τ.

In spacetime, nothing is truly stationary. An apple resting on Earth still travels forward through time. Every object moves through spacetime; its "speed" is simply distributed between space and time.

**Lecture 2: Velocity in Spacetime**

How, then, do we describe velocity in this four-dimensional spacetime?

The apple traces its worldline, described by coordinates like *t* and *r*. Yet these coordinates are just labels. Velocity requires more. At any point on the worldline, a tangent **vector** points in the direction of the apple's motion through spacetime. This is its **spacetime velocity**, denoted **v**.

The length, or magnitude, of this velocity vector is constant for all objects: the **speed of light**, *c*. We write this as ||**v**|| = *c*.

Physicists often simplify calculations by using units where *c* = 1 (measuring distance in light-seconds, for instance). In such units, ||**v**|| = 1, showing time as a measure of distance in spacetime.

We relate this spacetime velocity to our coordinates using **basis vectors**, **e**₀ and **e**₁, aligned with the coordinate axes (say, the *t* and *r* axes) at the point of interest. The velocity vector **v** can be written as a sum of these basis vectors, each scaled by a **component**: **v** = *v*⁰**e**₀ + *v*¹**e**₁.

The component *v*⁰ = d*t*/dτ shows how coordinate time *t* changes per unit of the apple's proper time τ. Likewise, *v*¹ = d*r*/dτ shows how the radial coordinate *r* changes per unit of τ.

**Einstein summation notation** simplifies writing sums over coordinates. Instead of **v** = *v*⁰**e**₀ + *v*¹**e**₁, we write **v** = *v*<sup>α</sup>**e**<sub>α</sub>. A repeated Greek index (like α), one superscript and one subscript, implies summation over all its possible values (here, 0 and 1).

Consider a satellite orbiting Earth, described by coordinate time *t* and angle φ. Its worldline spirals through spacetime. Its velocity vector has components *v*<sup>t</sup> = d*t*/dτ (temporal speed relative to proper time) and *v*<sup>φ</sup> = dφ/dτ (angular speed relative to proper time).

Can we find the magnitude *c* using the Pythagorean theorem on these components: (*v*<sup>t</sup>)² + (*v*<sup>φ</sup>)² = *c*²? No. This fails because coordinates are arbitrary and do not necessarily represent true distances. Two satellites at different heights might have the same angular speed component *v*<sup>φ</sup>, yet the outer one covers more distance and moves faster. The coordinate component *v*<sup>φ</sup> alone does not capture the true speed.

**Lecture 3: Geodesics - The Straightest Paths**

How, then, do we determine the path of an object moving freely? We explore **geodesics** – the straightest possible paths in curved spacetime.

On a flat surface, an object free from external forces follows a straight line. Its velocity vector remains unchanged as it moves along this path. On a curved surface, like a sphere, the straightest possible path is one where the velocity vector changes as little as possible relative to the surface itself. This path is a geodesic. On a sphere, these geodesics are great circles. The velocity vector, moving along a great circle, stays aligned with the surface's curve.

An object following a geodesic experiences no spacetime acceleration; its velocity vector is constant relative to spacetime's geometry. Mathematically, d**v**/dτ = 0 along a geodesic.

Recall that the velocity vector decomposes into components: **v** = *v*<sup>α</sup>**e**<sub>α</sub>. Taking the derivative with respect to proper time τ, using the product rule, yields: d**v**/dτ = (d*v*<sup>α</sup>/dτ)**e**<sub>α</sub> + *v*<sup>α</sup>(d**e**<sub>α</sub>/dτ).

The second term, *v*<sup>α</sup>(d**e**<sub>α</sub>/dτ), is crucial. In a curved coordinate system, or on a curved surface, the basis vectors **e**<sub>α</sub> themselves change direction as we move. The change in a basis vector **e**<sub>α</sub> depends on how it varies along each coordinate direction x<sup>μ</sup> and how fast we move in that direction (*v*<sup>μ</sup>): d**e**<sub>α</sub>/dτ = (∂**e**<sub>α</sub>/∂x<sup>μ</sup>) *v*<sup>μ</sup>. (Summation over μ is implied).

The rate of change of basis vector **e**<sub>α</sub> along coordinate direction x<sup>μ</sup>, namely ∂**e**<sub>α</sub>/∂x<sup>μ</sup>, is itself a vector. Its components in the basis **e**<sub>λ</sub> define the **Christoffel symbols**, Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub>: ∂**e**<sub>α</sub>/∂x<sup>μ</sup> = Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub> **e**<sub>λ</sub>. These symbols measure how the coordinate basis vectors twist and turn.

Substituting this back into the condition for zero acceleration (d**v**/dτ = 0) gives the **geodesic equation**:
d²x<sup>λ</sup>/dτ² + Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub> (dx<sup>μ</sup>/dτ)(dx<sup>α</sup>/dτ) = 0.
(Summation over α and μ is implied).

This equation describes how an object's coordinates x<sup>λ</sup> change as it follows a geodesic. The term d²x<sup>λ</sup>/dτ² is coordinate acceleration, while the term with the Christoffel symbols accounts for the changing coordinate system and spacetime curvature.

An airplane following a great circle (a geodesic) on Earth moves 'straight' relative to the spherical surface, yet its latitude and longitude coordinates change in a complex way, described by the geodesic equation with the appropriate Christoffel symbols for spherical coordinates.

Note that non-zero Christoffel symbols indicate the curvedness of the chosen *coordinate system*. Even flat spacetime can have non-zero Christoffel symbols if described using curved coordinates (like polar coordinates).

**Lecture 4: The Metric Tensor - Measuring Spacetime**

The geodesic equation requires the Christoffel symbols. How do we find them? The answer lies in the **metric tensor**, which defines the geometry of spacetime itself.

Recall that coordinate differences, like *dx*⁰ and *dx*¹, do not directly give the physical distance *ds* between nearby points if the coordinate system is distorted. The simple Pythagorean formula, (*ds*)² = (*dx*⁰)² + (*dx*¹)²?, fails unless the coordinates are Cartesian on a flat surface.

The general relationship between the squared distance (*ds*)² and small coordinate differences *dx*<sup>μ</sup> involves coefficients, *g*<sub>μν</sub>, organised into the **metric tensor**:

```markdown
[ g₀₀  g₀₁ ]
[ g₁₀  g₁₁ ]
```
(In 4D, this would be a 4x4 matrix).

Using Einstein notation (summing over repeated indices μ and ν), this relationship is concisely written as:
(*ds*)² = *g*<sub>μν</sub> *dx*<sup>μ</sup> *dx*<sup>ν</sup>.

The metric tensor, *g*<sub>μν</sub>, connects abstract coordinates to physical distances and angles.

For flat spacetime with Cartesian coordinates, the metric is the identity matrix ([1, 0], [0, 1]), yielding the standard Pythagorean theorem: (*ds*)² = (*dx*⁰)² + (*dx*¹)² (or the Minkowski equivalent in 4D). In polar coordinates (r, φ) on a flat plane, the metric is ([1, 0], [0, r²]), giving (*ds*)² = (*dr*)² + r²(*d*φ)². The *g*<sub>φφ</sub> = r² term shows that an angular step *d*φ corresponds to a larger distance *ds* when *r* is larger.

With the metric, we can correctly calculate the squared magnitude of the spacetime velocity vector **v** (which we know is *c*²): *c*² = *g*<sub>μν</sub> *v*<sup>μ</sup> *v*<sup>ν</sup>. This generalized Pythagorean theorem holds in any coordinate system for any spacetime geometry defined by *g*<sub>μν</sub>.

Furthermore, the metric tensor determines the Christoffel symbols. Since the metric defines the geometry and the coordinate system's properties, it contains the information needed to find how the basis vectors change. The Christoffel symbols Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub> can be calculated directly from the partial derivatives of the metric tensor components *g*<sub>μν</sub> using the formula:
Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub> = (1/2) *g*<sup>λρ</sup> (∂*g*<sub>ρα</sub>/∂x<sup>μ</sup> + ∂*g*<sub>ρμ</sub>/∂x<sup>α</sup> - ∂*g*<sub>μα</sub>/∂x<sup>ρ</sup>),
where *g*<sup>λρ</sup> is the inverse metric tensor. Knowing the metric tensor allows us to find the Christoffel symbols.

Thus, we have a clear path: the **Metric Tensor** (*g*<sub>μν</sub>) defines the geometry; from it, we compute the **Christoffel Symbols** (Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub>); these symbols enter the **Geodesic Equation**, which we solve to find the **Trajectories** of freely moving objects.

For instance, the metric for a sphere allows calculation of the Christoffel symbols needed to find great circles (geodesics). The simplest metric, the **Minkowski metric**, describes flat spacetime; its Christoffel symbols are all zero, leading to straight-line motion in Cartesian coordinates.

**Lecture 5: Einstein's Field Equations - Geometry Meets Matter**

What determines the metric tensor *g*<sub>μν</sub>? What shapes spacetime's geometry? The answer lies in the distribution of energy and momentum, described by the **Einstein field equations**.

The metric tensor *g*<sub>μν</sub> allows us to calculate distances and Christoffel symbols, defining the 'stage' of spacetime. But what dictates the form of this stage? Think of spacetime as a flexible sheet. Placing mass or energy onto it causes the sheet to curve. This illustrates the core idea: matter and energy curve spacetime.

Mathematically, how do we quantify curvature? On a flat surface, initially parallel lines stay parallel. On a curved surface, like a sphere, they converge or diverge – a sign of curvature. A precise measure involves parallel transport: slide a vector around a closed loop, keeping it parallel to itself at every step. On a flat surface, it returns unchanged. On a curved surface, its final direction differs from its initial one. This change reveals the curvature enclosed by the loop.

The **Riemann curvature tensor**, *R*<sup>ρ</sup><sub>σμν</sub>, quantifies this effect. It measures the change in a vector parallel-transported around a tiny loop and is calculated from the second derivatives of the metric tensor (and thus involves derivatives of the Christoffel symbols).

Contracting (summing over pairs of indices) the Riemann tensor yields the simpler **Ricci tensor**, *R*<sub>μν</sub>. It captures key aspects of curvature, relating to how volumes are distorted in curved spacetime. A further contraction gives the **Ricci scalar**, *R* = *g*<sup>μν</sup>*R*<sub>μν</sub>, a single number representing the average spacetime curvature at a point.

What causes this curvature? Energy and momentum do. Their distribution is described by the **energy-momentum tensor**, *T*<sub>μν</sub>. Its components represent physical quantities: *T*⁰⁰ is energy density, *T*⁰ⁱ is momentum density in the *i*-direction, *T*ⁱⁱ is pressure in the *i*-direction, and *T*ⁱʲ (for i ≠ j) represents shear stresses.

Einstein connected geometry and matter with his **field equations**:
*R*<sub>μν</sub> - (1/2)*R*g<sub>μν</sub> = (8πG/*c*⁴)*T*<sub>μν</sub>.

The left side, involving the Ricci tensor, Ricci scalar, and metric tensor, describes spacetime geometry. The right side, containing the energy-momentum tensor, describes the distribution of energy and momentum. The constant (8πG/*c*⁴) links the two, ensuring agreement with Newtonian gravity in weak fields.

As John Wheeler put it: "Spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve." The field equations express the latter; the geodesic equation expresses the former.

An alternative form of the field equations, derived by taking the trace, is *R*<sub>μν</sub> = (8πG/*c*⁴)(*T*<sub>μν</sub> - (1/2)*T*g<sub>μν</sub>), where *T* is the trace of the energy-momentum tensor.

These are ten coupled, nonlinear partial differential equations, notoriously difficult to solve. Exact solutions exist only for highly symmetric situations. The **Schwarzschild solution** is a key exact solution describing the spacetime outside a spherical, non-rotating mass like a star or black hole. Its metric tensor, in standard coordinates (t, r, θ, φ), is:
(*ds*)² = -(1 - 2GM/*c*²r)*c*²(*dt*)² + (1 - 2GM/*c*²r)<sup>-1</sup>(*dr*)² + r²(*dθ*)² + r²sin²(θ)(*dφ*)².

**Lecture 6: Solving Problems in General Relativity**

Let us now review the procedure for applying General Relativity and look at some examples. To solve a problem using General Relativity, we generally follow these steps:

1.  **Select the Metric Tensor (g<sub>μν</sub>):** Identify the spacetime geometry appropriate to the situation. Common metrics include Minkowski (flat spacetime), Schwarzschild (spherical mass), Kerr (rotating mass), or Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (homogeneous, isotropic universe).
2.  **Choose Coordinates (x<sup>μ</sup>):** Select a coordinate system that simplifies the problem, often exploiting symmetries (e.g., spherical coordinates for a spherical mass).
3.  **Use Symmetries:** Simplify the mathematics by identifying constants of motion (like conserved energy or angular momentum arising from symmetries in the metric).
4.  **Determine the Motion:** Two main paths exist:
    * Use the velocity norm: For massive particles, *g*<sub>μν</sub> *v*<sup>μ</sup> *v*<sup>ν</sup> = *c*² (using a convention where this is positive; sometimes -c² is used), relating velocity components.
    * Use the Geodesic Equation: d²x<sup>λ</sup>/dτ² + Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub>(dx<sup>μ</sup>/dτ)(dx<sup>α</sup>/dτ) = 0. This requires computing the Christoffel symbols Γ<sup>λ</sup><sub>μα</sub> from the metric *g*<sub>μν</sub>. For light, the norm is zero, and proper time τ is replaced by an affine parameter λ.

Consider time dilation for an astronaut on the International Space Station (ISS). We use the Schwarzschild metric in spherical coordinates. Assuming a circular orbit simplifies the problem (r=constant, θ=π/2). The velocity norm equation *c*² = *g*<sub>tt</sub>(*v*<sup>t</sup>)² + *g*<sub>φφ</sub>(*v*<sup>φ</sup>)² connects the rate of coordinate time passage d*t*/dτ (*v*<sup>t</sup>) to the known orbital speed and metric components. Solving yields *v*<sup>t</sup> = d*t*/dτ = (1 - 2GM/*c*²r)<sup>-1/2</sup> (1 - *v*²/ *c*² )<sup>-1/2</sup>, where *v* is the orbital speed. This value, being greater than 1, shows that coordinate time *t* (measured far away) passes faster than the astronaut's proper time τ.

Next, consider a satellite falling radially towards the Sun. Again, we use the Schwarzschild metric. For radial infall (θ, φ constant), the relevant geodesic equations simplify. The equation for the radial coordinate *r* becomes: d²r/dτ² = -GM/r² + (3GM/*c*²r²)(dr/dτ)². This describes the satellite's acceleration, including a relativistic correction to the Newtonian -GM/r² term.

Lastly, examine light bending near a massive object. Using the Schwarzschild metric and setting the motion within a plane (θ=π/2), we solve the geodesic equation for light. Since light follows null paths, we use an affine parameter λ instead of proper time τ, and the velocity norm is zero: *g*<sub>μν</sub>(dx<sup>μ</sup>/dλ)(dx<sup>ν</sup>/dλ) = 0. The resulting trajectory equation can be written as d²u/dφ² + u = 3GMu²/*c*², where u = 1/r. This describes how the light path bends from a straight line (d²u/dφ² + u = 0).

These examples show how General Relativity accurately describes phenomena from time dilation and orbital mechanics to the bending of light by gravity. General Relativity provides a framework for understanding gravity as the geometry of spacetime, shaped by matter and energy – a profound view of the cosmos.
