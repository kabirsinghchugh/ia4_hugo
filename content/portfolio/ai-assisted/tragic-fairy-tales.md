In a moment of audacious arrogance, he ordered the servants to turn the bed around. In an instant, Death was standing at the King’s “head.” The physician administered the herb. The King was saved.
Death was furious. He appeared to his godson, his eyes burning with cold fire. He had been cheated. Tricked by a cheap parlor game. “For this once,” Death hissed, “because you are my godson, I will forgive it. But if you ever dare to do such a thing again, your own life is the price.”
The warning was given. The physician, shaken, agreed. But the poison of pride, once tasted, is not easily purged.
Soon after, the King’s only daughter, a princess of legendary beauty, fell gravely ill. The heartbroken King made a proclamation: whoever could save her would have her hand in marriage and inherit the kingdom. The physician went to her bedside. He saw her beauty, pale and fading, and he was lost. And he saw his godfather, standing at the foot of her bed, his bony finger pointing down, his expression final.
The physician did not see the warning. He did not see the rage in Death’s hollow eyes. He saw only the princess, the crown, the life he could steal. He did not even bother to turn the bed. He simply administered the herb, a direct act of defiance. He chose the girl over the law.
The instant color returned to the princess’s cheeks, a cold hand of bone seized the physician’s arm. He was dragged from the room, through the floor, down into the belly of the earth.
He stood in a vast, dark cavern. As far as the eye could see, there were millions of candles, flickering. Some were tall and burned brightly. Others were half-gone. Many were tiny stumps, sputtering on the verge of extinguishing. “This,” Death said, his voice echoing in the immense silence, “is the silent, guttering library of mankind. Each candle is a life.”
He led his godson to a small, pathetic stub of a candle, its flame barely clinging to the wick. “This one,” Death said, “is yours.”
Terror seized the physician. “Godfather!” he begged. “Light a new one for me! Please, I beg you!”
“I cannot,” Death replied, his voice flat, devoid of all pity. “A candle must go out before a new one can be lit.”
“Then put my old one on top of a new one!” the physician cried, desperate, bargaining for a few more moments of life. “So it can continue when it is finished!”
Death looked at him. He picked up the tiny, sputtering stump of wax. “You wish for me to mend this?” he asked. He made a show of trying to place it on a new, tall candle. But his bony fingers were so clumsy. A clumsy little gesture… and the candle stump was dropped. It hit the floor and the tiny flame went out.
And in the darkness of the cavern, the physician fell dead to the ground.
The Legacy
This is the final truth. This chronicle is not a tragedy. It is an execution. It is the last word on the nature of rules. It teaches that you can be favored by the greatest power there is, but you are still subject to its laws. Death's one virtue is that he is the same for all. His one law is that his judgment is final. The moment you believe you are clever enough to cheat the house, the house always, always wins. The physician’s sin was not greed, nor was it love. It was the arrogance to believe that the rules that applied to everyone else did not apply to him. And his godfather, the one true egalitarian, simply corrected the error.
