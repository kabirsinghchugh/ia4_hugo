## 1. Loosen the Old Strings

- Use the string winder to loosen your guitar strings by tuning them down. You'll want to remove all the tension so they don't ping when you cut them.

## 2. Cut the Old Strings in Half

- Take your wire cutters and cut the strings in half. This makes them much easier to remove.

## 3. Remove the Old Strings

- Push the bottom half of the cut strings out through the back of the bridge.
- Unwind the top half of the strings from the tuning post, but be careful because these strings can be a bit pointy.

## 4. Clean Your Guitar's Fretboard

- Now that the strings are off, it's the perfect time to clean your guitar's fretboard.

## 5. Insert the New Strings

- Feed each one into the bridge of the guitar, from the thickest in the low E position all the way to the thinnest in the high e position.
- If you have a through body guitar bridge like on a Stratocaster, make sure that the string enters through the back plate into the tremolo block and exits through the center of the saddle.
- Pull it through until the ball end is in full contact with the bridge for guitars.
- With the stop tail bridge like a Les Paul, simply pass the string through the back of the bridge, again pulling it through until the ball end is in contact with the bridge.

## 6. Feed the Strings through the Tuning Pegs

- This next step depends on whether you have standard or vintage style tuners.
- For standard style tuners, turn the machine head so that the hole in the tuning post faces the neck.
- Then pass the string all the way through it and hold it so that it's tight.
- Pinch the string at the second fret, pull it back to the third fret, and make a sharp bend where the excess leaves the tuning post.
  - This gives you enough slack to wind around the post without giving you too much, which can affect the tuning stability.
- **Vintage style tuners:** pull the string so that it runs alongside the desired tuning post and hold it tight, then pinch the string at the second fret, pull it back to the third, and clip the excess where it passes the tuning post. Insert the string into the top of the tuning post and bend it into the slot.

## 7. Begin Winding the Strings

- Hold the string in place and begin tightening so that the string is sat on the inside of the tuning post. For inline tuners this means turning them anti-clockwise.
- **Split head socket:**
  - Turn the side with the thicker strings anti-clockwise and the thinner strings clockwise.
  - London style tuners: make sure that the excess of the string passes under the first wind then over the rest of the winds. This squishes the string into place, again making the tuning more stable. Make sure that the string forms an even coil that moves downwards on the tuning post. Before it's fully tightened, rub a pencil where the string comes into contact with the saddle and nut; this lubricates the nut and saddle, which provides better tuning stability and helps to prevent snapping. Finally, tighten the string so it sits in the correct nut slot and is resting on the saddle. Repeat for all strings until each of them has just enough tension to ring out when plucked.

## 8. Tune the New Strings

- Tune each string.
  - Your strings need to stretch out and your bridge and neck will need to adjust.
  - It takes about four or five rounds of tuning for the strings to become stable.

## 9. Cut Off the String Excess

- Finally, cut off the excess string that's past the tuning post.
- Pay attention to not accidentally cut your new string below the tuning post, otherwise you'll have to replace the whole string.
- You've now restrung your guitar.
