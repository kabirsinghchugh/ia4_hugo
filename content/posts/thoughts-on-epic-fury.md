# Thoughts on Operation "Epic Fury"

Over the past couple of weeks, there has been a lingering sense of unease creeping up on me, ever since Israel and the United States launched "Operation Epic Fury" at the end of February. I have been trying to reflect upon the unease, and articulate it over the last two weeks, and despite the facts being so clear and upfront - It took two weeks for my mind to realize the horrific truth that left me a little stunned.   

Why stunned? Because even the harshest of  critics of the "operation" seemingly missed out or simply bypassed one little detail to leave it as a footnote at best - which is it? well if have to question that, then that answers my hypothesis. 

Before I proceed, I just want to  clarify upfront: _I'm not here to defend Iran's government or fish out the "good guys" and the "bad guys". I'm asking something individual, personal and, I think, more uncomfortable truth that even the most critical of commentators in America still managed to sidestep the significance of the moral  responsibility and conscience  that comes with acknowledgement of the reality of the fact._


## **The criticism exists. It's  just -   _weirdly contained_? **

Of course there hasn't  been any shortage of serious people pushing back. Here are some pushbacks that I have come across over the last two weeks -

-  No long-term plan or duration estimate — A vaguely articulated campaign that could drag on for either a few days to four years without a clear endgame. 
-  The War isn't going the way the White House sold it and the public knows it.
-  Even conservative Americans have argued that it is a betrayal of **America First**  or **No New Wars** principles. 
-  UN and some World Leaders have called the strikes **unlawful**.

So the criticism is real. Loud, even.

But what's been eating at me is the nature of criticsm.  Almost all of it is *strategic-level* criticism. 

You hear the words "exit strategies", "international law", "alliance frictions", economic loss, gas prices, competence of the President and his staff - there's  enough of that everywhere. 

> Even the most passionate anti-war voices in mainstream circles are essentially basically arguing: *Yeah this war was done badly.*

**And that framing** — "done badly" — quietly smuggles in an assumption I don't think anyone's examined closely enough.

---

**The Minab school massacre.**

On February 28th, a U.S. Tomahawk missile struck the *Shajareh Tayyebeh* elementary school in Minab, Iran. **Over 170 schoolgirls died.**

The White House's initial response was to suggest it was an *Iranian misfire*. (Essentially saying that it was Iran's own incompetence). When that fell apart and it was confirmed beyond doubt that it was a U.S. missile that was the cause — the story shifted to what was now a  **targeting error** due to Outdated map data. (The building had previously been associated with a military base facility). The Pentagon expressed regret.

Roughly 48 hours later, the news cycle moved on.

Who was fired? Where is the international outcry? Where are the tribunals? The answer to the question no one asked the U.S. in the first place for why little children have to die, was answered with the most rational --  *logistics of war* — unfortunate collateral damage. To just realise the weight of this, and it's not the first incident, but one in which has become public knowledge of who was the aggressor, no justifications, no answers. Yes the U.S. did say "Are we the bad guys?" but in their failure to address this, they raise the moral weight of the question more than they realize.

Ever since I've been staring at that coverage, I keep coming back to a single question --  *what would have  happened if it were the other way round ?*

> An Iranian cruise missile strikes an elementary school in Columbus, Ohio killing 170 American girls aged 7 to 12.

I don't need to describe the media response in detail, because you already know it. You can probably feel it in your gut right now  reading that hypothetical. 

Even so, it is useful to imagine :- 

1. Every SINGLE name would be on television. Parents would be on every network, weeping. 
2. The President would that very night give an Oval Office address promising justice and retaliation in an emotionally solemn manner
3. The word "terrorism", "barbarism", "humanity", "innocence" would be used within minutes. The whole nation would condemn the Evil. What they did was an "Act of war", the President would declare. 
4. And **now**,  suppose Iran's response to that outcry is: \
\
> *"We relied on outdated GPS data. The intended target was a logistics hub three miles away. We regret the collateral loss of life. We are conducting an internal investigation."*


Would that be accepted/acceptable? Would American commentators say, "Well,it's unfortunate but that's war, you know?"  Imagine the salt rubbed when the international discussion is more about how the incident was a logistical embarrassment* which could  hurt Iran's "image/PR" in geopolitics.

The American political and media class would erupt at this grotesque and  malicious
 insult. It would be cited as evidence of the evil Iranian contempt for human life and decency.

And... yet, here we are. That is almost verbatim what Washington offered the world after Minab. And the world (or at least the Western world) — largely accepted it and moved on. 

### **Why does this happen? I don't think it's malicious. I think it's structural.**

- Critics like Peter Beinart and Mehdi Hasan have pointed to the "American Exceptionalism trap" -- the tendency to judge your own side by its intentions and everyone else by the impact of their actions.

>  We didn't *mean* to kill those children, so it's a tragedy. If they killed our children, intent would be irrelevant; impact would be everything.

- There's also what media analysts call the "worthy versus unworthy victim" framing. \
\
It's not that editors consciously decide Iranian children matter less. It's that Iranian children(and the non-western world) arrive in the news pre-sorted and categorised as a faceless, contextless abstraction. "The millions being a statistic - the human cost of conflict." \
\
American children arrive as individuals, for the rest-of-the-world! People from Sudan to Bangladesh would be made to learn about the deaths — their names, their potential, their videos,  hobbies and futures, even as they simply work to exist for the next meal. 

I know it's an AI cliche by now, but anyway, It's not cruelty. It's just the nature of humanity. The very thing when seriously confronted led to Ivan Karamazov rebel against God in Dostoevsky's Brothers Karamazov, and ultimately to the moral culpability in letting it happen, even implicitly, and to the Narrator in Albert Camus' 'The Fall'. The strange thing is that the government is brazen enough to quickly contradict themselves, engage in cheap talk and non-sensical behaviour, and still, the world's most "advanced country" can do anything, and unlike earlier, doesn't even need to hide what they are doing anymore. That's on our conscience - for not speaking our mind when we should. For not thinking for ourselves when we must. 

The "competence trap" is maybe the worst one, though. When the loudest critique of a war is that it lacks an exit strategy, you've already conceded the legitimacy of the violence. You've turned a moral question into a management problem - Killing of children versus rising oil prices- the former is inconsequential, the latter pivotal. And that's human nature. The problem arises when 

1. You still buy into the moral posturing -- it's machiavellian if you know and foolish if you don't.
2. American exceptionalism breaking down in 21st century - military and economically -- American military no matter how advanced, can be deterred by the poorest nation with an atomic. And it is becoming clearer to more nations the need for deterrence and independence  because of American actions.

## TLDR

I expected the Trump administration to defend this war. I expected MAGA commentators to wave it away. That's not surprising.

What genuinely shocked me was realising that even the critics - the serious, well-credentialed, genuinely concerned critics - mostly stopped short of the actual question. The question isn't whether the U.S. had a plan. The question is whether the deaths of those 170 girls registered, *emotionally and morally*, the way they would have if they were American girls, just to give a sense of the weight of crimes America is inflicting upon the world.

And I don't think they did. I think most of the Western coverage - even the critical coverage - processed Minab as an "embarrassment" , a "tactical error."  or at most "a tragedy" Not as 170 individual human beings with faces, and lives and potential and hobbies -  who went to school one morning but never came back home.


### FINAL DISCLAIMER - Not hating on America

I repeat - **I'm not simply hating on America. It's a question at the individual human level. Picture Columbus, Ohio. Picture the names on the news. Picture the silence that would fall over American life. Then picture Minab. Picture what actually happened. Picture the 48-hour news cycle. 

If those two pictures produce different feelings in you and they will, because you're human and proximity and familiarity are powerful , - I think that gap is worth sitting with. Not because you're a bad person for feeling it. But because that gap, multiplied across an entire culture, is how wars like this become possible. How they become repeatable. How 170 children can die and the main debate becomes whether the targeting software needs an upgrade.

That's all. I just think it's worth looking at directly, without flinching away into strategy talk.
