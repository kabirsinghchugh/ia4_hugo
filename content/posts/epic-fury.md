I want to be clear upfront: this is a thought experiment. I'm not here to defend Iran's government or relitigate who the "good guys" are. I'm asking something narrower and, I think, more uncomfortable — why does even the *most critical* American commentary on this war still manage to sidestep the one question that would make most people deeply uncomfortable?



**The criticism exists. It's just... weirdly contained.**

Since Operation Epic Fury launched in February, there's been no shortage of serious people pushing back. Alex Vatanka at the Middle East Institute warned early that there's no long-term political project here — just a campaign that could drag on for "four months or four years" with no clear endgame. Suzanne Maloney at Brookings has been pretty blunt that the war isn't going the way the White House sold it. Even Megyn Kelly and Matt Walsh — not exactly war-skeptic archetypes — called it a betrayal of America First principles. Bernie Sanders and Ro Khanna are pushing a War Powers Resolution alongside Thomas Massie, which is about as bipartisan as Congress gets these days. UN special rapporteurs have called the strikes unlawful.

So the criticism is real. Loud, even.

But here's the thing that's been eating at me: almost all of it is *strategic* criticism. It's about exit strategies, international law violations, alliance frictions, economic blowback, whether the targeting intelligence was competent enough. Even the most passionate anti-war voices in mainstream circles are essentially arguing: *this war was done badly.*

And that framing — "done badly" — quietly smuggles in an assumption I don't think anyone's examined closely enough.

---

**The Minab school.**

On February 28th, a U.S. Tomahawk missile struck the Shajareh Tayyebeh elementary school in Minab, Iran. Over 170 schoolgirls died.

The White House's initial response was to suggest it was an Iranian misfire. Their own incompetence, essentially. When that fell apart — the NYT investigative team confirmed it was a U.S. missile, not Iranian — the story became a "targeting error." Outdated maps. The building had previously been associated with a base facility. The Pentagon expressed regret.

And then, roughly 48 hours later, the news cycle moved on.

No one was fired. No tribunal was proposed. The story shifted to logistics — how the map was wrong — rather than to the 170 children who no longer exist.

I've been staring at that coverage, and I keep coming back to a single question: *what would happen if the situation were reversed?*

---

**The thought experiment. Please actually sit with this.**

Imagine an Iranian cruise missile strikes an elementary school in Columbus, Ohio. 170 American girls are killed.

I don't need to describe the media response in detail, because you already know it. You can feel it in your gut right now just reading that hypothetical. Every name would be on television. Parents would be on every network, weeping. The President would interrupt everything for an Oval Office address. The word "terrorism" would be used within minutes — not "targeting error," not "kinetic event," not "collateral loss." Terrorism. Evil. An act of war.

Now here's the part of the thought experiment that I think actually matters:

Imagine Iran's response to that outcry was: *"We relied on outdated GPS data. The intended target was a logistics hub three miles away. We regret the collateral loss of life. We are conducting an internal investigation."*

Would that be accepted? Would American commentators say, "Well, mistakes happen in the fog of war"? Would pundits debate whether this "hurts Iran's strategic image" rather than discussing the dead children?

You already know the answer. The American political and media class would treat that statement as a grotesque insult. It would be mocked. It would be cited as evidence of Iranian contempt for human life.

And yet. That is almost verbatim what Washington offered the world after Minab. And the world — or at least the Western part of it — largely accepted it as sufficient.

---

Why does this happen?

Critics like Peter Beinart and Mehdi Hasan have pointed to something they call the "American Exceptionalism trap" — the tendency to judge your own side by its intentions and everyone else by the impact of their actions. We didn't *mean* to kill those children, so it's a tragedy. If they killed our children, intent would be irrelevant; impact would be everything.

There's also what media analysts call the "worthy versus unworthy victim" framing. It's not that editors consciously decide Iranian children matter less. It's that Iranian children arrive in the news already pre-sorted into a category: context, statistics, "the human cost of conflict." American children would arrive as individuals — with names and hobbies and futures. The difference in coverage isn't cruelty. It's habit. A deeply embedded habit.

The "competence trap" is maybe the worst one, though. When the loudest critique of a war is that it lacks an exit strategy, you've already conceded the legitimacy of the violence. You've turned a moral question — do we have the right to do this? — into a management question — are we doing it efficiently enough? And that shift happens so fast, so automatically, that most people don't notice it.
 ---

I expected the Trump administration to defend this war. I expected MAGA commentators to wave it away. That's not surprising.

What shocked me was realising that even the critics — the serious, well-credentialed, genuinely concerned critics — mostly stopped short of the actual question. The question isn't whether the U.S. had a plan. The question is whether the deaths of those 170 girls registered, *emotionally and morally*, the way they would have if they were American girls.

And I don't think they did. I think most of the Western coverage — even the critical coverage — processed Minab as a "setback" or an "embarrassment" or a "tactical error." Not as 170 individual human beings who went to school one morning and didn't come home.

---

try the mirror test. Not as a political exercise. As an honest emotional one.

Picture Columbus, Ohio. Picture the names on the news. Picture the silence that would fall over American life.

Then picture Minab. Picture what actually happened. Picture the 48-hour news cycle.

If those two pictures produce different feelings in you — and they will, because you're human and proximity and familiarity are powerful — I think that gap is worth sitting with. Not because you're a bad person for feeling it. But because that gap, multiplied across an entire culture, is how wars like this become possible. How they become repeatable. How 170 children can die and the main debate becomes whether the targeting software needs an upgrade.

That's all. I just think it's worth looking at directly, without flinching away into strategy talk.
